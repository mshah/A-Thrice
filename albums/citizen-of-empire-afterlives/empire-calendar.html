Empire Calendar

“Empire Calendar” is a celebratory but historically unsentimental song about the collapse of the British Empire and the global wave of decolonization that followed World War II.

Listen / buy this song on Bandcamp ↗
About this song

“Empire Calendar” is a celebratory but historically unsentimental song about the collapse of the British Empire and the global wave of decolonization that followed World War II.

The song frames decolonization as a recurring administrative event: every year, dozens of former British colonies celebrate independence days that act like calendar reminders of imperial retreat. This framing is deeply ironic. Empires often recorded rule through paperwork, maps, census systems, and legal archives; the song turns that bureaucratic instinct against empire itself. The empire kept records—but the colonized kept dates.

Musically, the track combines danceable call-and-response hooks with dense historical references, creating a tension between celebration and grief. The song insists that independence matters and deserves celebration, while also acknowledging that many exits from empire were messy, violent, and incomplete. Partition, border disputes, military basing, forced migration, and inherited conflict remain part of the imperial afterlife.

The central satirical hook connects Brexit to decolonization. Britain’s departure from the European Union was often framed as reclaiming sovereignty and “taking back control.” The song highlights the irony that dozens of countries fought much harder for sovereignty from Britain itself.

One of the song’s most unusual structural features is its roll call of countries. The countries are not random: they are listed in chronological order of independence from Britain, transforming the chorus breaks into a living historical timeline of imperial unraveling.

Artist’s annotations

Choose an underlined lyric, or open an annotation below.

01“Recurring annual event detected”

The song opens with an intentionally bureaucratic administrative voice, as though decolonization were a recurring calendar reminder inside scheduling software.

This reflects a major A-Thrice motif: systems and bureaucracy becoming narrators of human history.

The irony is that something as emotionally charged as anti-colonial struggle is framed like Outlook or Google Calendar metadata.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
02“Budget allocation: Insufficient”

This line works on multiple levels.

Literal reading: Britain lacked the money to sustain empire after World War II.

Satirical reading: there is not enough “budget” to process the sheer scale of imperial collapse.

Historical reading: postwar Britain was financially weakened, deeply indebted, and increasingly unable to maintain expensive colonial administration, military deployment, and suppression.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
03“War won, treasury emptied”

Britain emerged from World War II on the winning side but financially exhausted.

The war left Britain heavily indebted, reliant on American loans, and struggling to finance both reconstruction and empire.

This line introduces one of the song’s central arguments: empire did not primarily collapse because Britain became morally anti-imperial—it became too expensive to maintain.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
04“The sun never set, but it ran up quite a claim”

This references the phrase:

“The sun never sets on the British Empire.”

The lyric turns the famous boast into financial satire.

“Claim” carries multiple meanings:

  • financial debt
  • legal liability
  • historical accountability

The empire’s global scale became both its prestige and its burden.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
05“Self-rule had the whole world listening”

This references the postwar rise of anti-colonial nationalism and the global legitimacy of self-determination.

After WWII, colonial rule became increasingly difficult to justify internationally, especially with the rise of the UN and Cold War geopolitics.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
06“They called it transfer”

British decolonization was often described in administrative language:

  • transfer of power
  • constitutional transition
  • handover

The song critiques this euphemistic language for sanitizing the mass movements, protests, strikes, imprisonment, and resistance that forced imperial retreat.

A major A-Thrice motif is institutional passive voice.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
07“Granting them permission to reclaim their land”

This is pure bureaucratic irony.

The line satirizes the absurd notion that an empire “grants permission” to people to possess land that was originally theirs.

The phrase exposes the arrogance embedded in colonial legal logic.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
08“Suez changed the lighting on the imperial stage”

This refers to the Suez Crisis (1956), often considered the clearest sign that Britain was no longer an independent imperial superpower.

Britain and France attempted to retake the Suez Canal after Egypt nationalized it, but pressure from the United States and Soviet Union forced withdrawal.

“Changed the lighting” is theatrical metaphor: the empire remained on stage, but everyone could now see it differently.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
09“Washington turned the page”

Britain could no longer act independently of American geopolitical interests.

The United States effectively signaled that the era of traditional European empire was ending.

The lyric continues the song’s calendar/page-turning motif.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
10“Retreat became statesmanship in ceremonial dress”

This critiques how imperial withdrawal was reframed as dignified diplomacy.

Rather than acknowledging loss of power, retreat could be narrated as strategic wisdom or benevolent transition.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
11“Britain left Europe / And called it control”

This references Brexit.

The lyric critiques sovereignty rhetoric by comparing Brexit to anti-colonial independence struggles.

A-Thrice uses irony rather than direct partisan commentary: the line points out historical symmetry.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
12“Half the world left Britain / Generations ago”

This is the song’s central thesis.

Dozens of countries fought for sovereignty from Britain long before Brexit.

The line reframes Brexit as one entry in a much longer global history of political separation.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
13“United States / The original Brexit”

This deliberately provocative joke frames the American Revolution as the first major British “exit.”

The comparison is intentionally playful but structurally meaningful: both involve sovereignty, governance, taxation, and political separation from London.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
14“Salt in the hand”

This references Gandhi’s Salt March (1930).

The campaign protested British taxation and monopoly over salt production and became one of the defining acts of Indian anti-colonial resistance.

Salt becomes a symbol of mass civil disobedience.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
15“Quit India rising”

Refers to the Quit India Movement of 1942, one of the most significant anti-colonial mass movements in British India.

The movement demanded immediate British withdrawal.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
16“Sailors breaking rank”

Refers to the 1946 Royal Indian Navy mutiny.

This event alarmed British officials because it suggested colonial military loyalty was fracturing.

This helped convince British leadership that long-term control of India was becoming impossible.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
17“Midnight raised two flags”

India and Pakistan became independent in August 1947.

The “two flags” represent simultaneous independence and partition.

This compresses liberation and division into one image.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
18“Freedom met Partition—border through the heart”

This references Partition of India.

Millions were displaced, and mass sectarian violence followed the drawing of borders between India and Pakistan.

“Heart” is both metaphorical and geographic.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
19“Mau Mau in the hills”

Refers to the Mau Mau uprising in Kenya (1952–1960).

British colonial authorities responded with detention camps, torture, and violent suppression.

The song emphasizes that anti-colonial resistance was often criminalized by empire.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
20“Emergency law made resistance into crime”

Colonial emergency powers allowed resistance movements to be reframed as criminal rather than political.

This connects to a recurring A-Thrice motif:

legitimacy versus criminality.

Power often determines which violence is called lawful.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
21“They organized”

This line rejects the narrative that freedom was benevolently granted.

Independence movements succeeded through organizing, protest, insurgency, labor action, and political coordination.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
22“Ireland learned the pattern before it crossed the sea”

The song argues colonial methods used throughout the empire had earlier precedents in Ireland.

These include:

  • plantation settlement
  • land seizure
  • sectarian division
  • partition

Ireland becomes a prototype of imperial governance.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
23“One island, two jurisdictions”

Ireland remains politically divided between:

  • Republic of Ireland
  • Northern Ireland (part of the UK)

This complicates clean independence narratives.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
24“Not every empire exit earns a date of its own”

Not all imperial endings produce clean independence holidays.

Some exits remain incomplete, contested, or politically unresolved.

This is one of the song’s most emotionally complex lines.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
25“Cyprus changed the banner; Britain kept its bases there”

Cyprus gained independence in 1960, but Britain retained sovereign military bases:

  • Akrotiri
  • Dhekelia

This illustrates a recurring theme:

formal independence does not always mean complete strategic withdrawal.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
26“Malaya left the empire; Malaysia formed the frame”

The Federation of Malaya became independent in 1957.

Malaysia was formed in 1963 by combining Malaya, Sabah, Sarawak, and Singapore.

The song distinguishes decolonization from later state formation.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
27“Singapore joined the union, then stepped outside the name”

Singapore joined Malaysia in 1963 but separated in 1965.

This illustrates how postcolonial state formation remained unstable even after imperial withdrawal.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
28“Status: independent. Borders: unresolved.”

The administrative bridge satirizes bureaucratic reporting language.

Even after independence, many colonial borders remained contested or artificial.

The bridge emphasizes the disconnect between formal status and lived reality.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
29“Liability: No record found”

This may be the song’s sharpest bureaucratic joke.

Administrative systems often meticulously preserve procedural records while obscuring responsibility.

The empire recorded borders, taxes, laws, and transfers—

but often leaves no official liability for harm.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
30“Freedom got a morning / Grief inherited the date”

This contains a double meaning.

Morning:

the dawn of independence.

Mourning:

grief, loss, displacement, violence.

The song argues independence anniversaries can hold both joy and trauma simultaneously.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
31“Independence tells you when— / Not always who you are”

A date can mark sovereignty.

It cannot automatically resolve identity, belonging, ethnicity, or nationhood.

This is one of the philosophical cores of the song.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
32“History can hold the sorrow / Without swallowing the light”

This is the emotional thesis of the bridge.

The song rejects two extremes:

  • triumphalist historical simplification
  • fatalistic focus on trauma alone

Both celebration and grief can coexist.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
33“Pakistan! India!” / “Sudan! Ghana!” / “Tanzania! Jamaica!”

The roll calls are a major structural device.

The countries are listed in chronological order of independence from Britain, creating a rhythmic historical timeline.

The roll calls function as:

  • chant
  • celebration
  • historical archive
  • collective memory
  • administrative counter-record

They transform history into communal sound.

Examples include:

1776 — United States

1919 — Afghanistan

1932 — Iraq

1946 — Jordan

1947 — Pakistan, India

1948 — Myanmar, Sri Lanka

1956 — Sudan

1957 — Ghana, Malaya

1960 onward — accelerating wave of decolonization

Each shouted country name becomes both a historical marker and a celebratory drum hit.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
34“You kept the records— / We remembered everything”

This is the song’s final thesis.

Empire preserved archives, legal files, census records, and administrative systems.

But the colonized preserved memory.

Records track governance.

Memory tracks meaning.

This opposition between official record and lived memory is central to A-Thrice’s worldview.

Further artist notes
  • The entire song is structured like a calendar app mixed with imperial paperwork.
  • Administrative language functions as satire throughout.
  • The repeated phrase “Turn the page” has multiple meanings:
  • calendar page
  • historical chapter
  • government file
  • turning away from empire
  • inability to stop history
  • The song intentionally refuses to become anti-celebratory.

It acknowledges partition, borders, and trauma without denying the legitimacy of independence celebrations.

  • A core A-Thrice theme appears throughout:

systems can record events,

but systems cannot fully contain human meaning.

  • This song connects strongly to other Citizen of Empire tracks such as “Glass Case Alibi,” where bureaucracy similarly becomes a mechanism for obscuring moral responsibility.
