Mother Tongue in a Minor Key

“Mother Tongue in a Minor Key” is a song about inherited language after fluency thins.

Listen / buy this song on Bandcamp ↗
About this song

“Mother Tongue in a Minor Key” is a song about inherited language after fluency thins. It is not a simple nostalgia song about “forgetting your roots.” It is more precise than that. The song asks what remains when vocabulary fades but rhythm, tone, prayer, warning, domestic atmosphere, and family weather remain in the body.

The title matters because the song is not claiming total loss. It is claiming transformation. The “mother tongue” is still present, but no longer always as literal speech. It survives in sound before meaning, in pitch before translation, in household habits before formal grammar, in prayer through the wall, in warning systems, and in the emotional climate of the home.

That makes it one of the deepest inheritance songs in this body of work. It is about East African Asian / Gujarati diaspora memory, but not in museum terms. It is about language after migration, after renaming, after papers, after adaptation — and about how the body may keep what the mouth sets down.

01“Mother tongue”

The phrase usually means a first language or native language, but the song expands it. Here “mother tongue” also means domestic atmosphere, maternal instruction, inherited sound, prayer, and emotional cadence. The tongue is both literal speech and an entire house-shaped way of being.

02“in a minor key”

This is one of the song’s most important phrases. “Minor key” suggests sadness, haunting, restraint, and emotional undertow. It implies that inheritance is still present, but not brightly or triumphantly. It arrives shaded, altered, intimate, and slightly unresolved.

Why the title works

The title says the language has not vanished. It has changed register. The song’s core claim is not “I lost it.” It is “I hear it differently now.”

03“Kitchen light”

The song begins in the domestic sphere, not in public performance. That matters. This is not heritage staged for outsiders. It is home-space: ordinary, practical, intimate, and lit from within.

04“Prayer in the next room”

05“Nauv-kar through the wall”

This refers to the Navkar Mantra in Jain tradition, one of the most important devotional recitations in the household world the song is evoking. The song’s pronunciation note matters because it is not treating prayer as text alone. It is remembering how the sound lived in the house.

16“pesa, duka, makai”
  • These are Swahili/Arabic-influenced words embedded in everyday family language:
  • pesa = money
  • duka = shop/store
  • The word makāī is shared across several Indo-Aryan languages (including Hindi, Punjabi, Sindhi, and Marathi, where it appears as makkā / makāī). It is generally traced to terms related to Mecca (Makkā in Arabic/Hindustani), as corn was historically introduced to parts of India via foreign trade routes and ports linked with West Asia/Portuguese contact in the 16th century, leading to folk or historical designations tied to imported grain names.

Their presence matters because they show how casually migration history can live inside household speech. The words have become ordinary, almost unnoticed, until another ear hears the shoreline in them.

39“Jam-bo Baa-na”

This is the phonetic rendering of Jambo Bwana, the well-known Swahili phrase/song line meaning “Hello, sir” or more loosely “Hello, mister / hello, boss.” In the song it functions less as literal greeting and more as remembered sound moving through family space and East African memory.

Why include “Jam-bo Baa-na”

The phonetic spelling matters for the same reason as “Nauv-kar”: this is a song about how sound survives in the body and ear, not just how words appear on a standardized page.
