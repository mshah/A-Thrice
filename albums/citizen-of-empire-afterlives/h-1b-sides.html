About this song

“H-1B-Sides” begins as a joke about one of the strangest forms of racial recognition: being recognized immediately, but recognized wrong.

The title combines the American H-1B work-visa category with the musical idea of a B-side—the less obvious track that sits outside the commercially familiar single. That pun contains the entire song. American culture hears “Indian” and loads a familiar playlist of characters: convenience-store worker, cab driver, doctor, programmer, H-1B engineer. The narrator responds that those roles are not necessarily false; what is false is treating them as the whole record.

The original writing concept was explicitly aimed at the stereotype rather than the immigrant. It described the target as America’s “prefab stereotype,” “cubicle caricature,” and “dropdown-menu imagination”: a huge diaspora compressed into a few occupational shortcuts. As Citizen of Empire expanded into Empire Infrastructure, the song acquired another layer. The current album documentation describes it as a song about “paperwork, professional legibility, classed mobility” and “the paperwork of being processed into value.”

That evolution explains the song’s structure. Verse 1 is funny because the stereotypes initially appear as sitcom clichés and corporate shorthand. Verse 2 opens the narrator’s actual archive—Kenya, Britain, the United States, protectorate history, migration. The bridge and Verse 3 then reveal the more dangerous side of the same logic: the supposedly desirable “smart,” “skilled,” hardworking immigrant can be recoded as excess population, housing pressure or economic threat when conditions change.

That makes “H-1B-Sides” part of Citizen of Empire’s larger argument about classification. The album repeatedly asks what happens when institutions convert people into fields, categories, statuses and useful functions. Here, race behaves like autocomplete: the system sees one piece of information and confidently predicts the rest.

The song’s final answer is not that the narrator is impossible to understand. It is simpler: the preset is too small for the person.

01“H-1B-side”

The title is the song’s master pun.

An H-1B is a U.S. temporary work-visa category associated with specialty occupations and has become culturally associated with highly skilled professional immigration, particularly technology.

A B-side is the secondary side of a record single—the track that was not chosen as the obvious commercial centerpiece. “Deep cut” extends the same record-store metaphor later in the song.

The narrator therefore casts herself as the H-1B-side: the version of South Asian identity the listener failed to hear because they kept playing the stereotypical single.

There is also an irony. The narrator is not actually saying, “I am an H-1B worker.” The visa itself becomes shorthand for America's tendency to read brown identity through occupational and immigration categories.

That distinction was present from the earliest song concept: the joke is aimed at the prefab stereotype, not H-1B immigrants themselves.

02“You heard ‘Indian,’ queued Apu again”

“Queued” turns racial stereotyping into software or media-player behavior.

Apu, the Indian convenience-store character associated with The Simpsons, became one of the most recognizable Indian caricatures in American popular culture. The lyric suggests the listener does not actually encounter the person in front of them; they simply load the familiar character again.

That makes “queued” important. The stereotype is not discovered through observation. It is already waiting in memory.

This begins the song’s software motif:

input: Indian

autocomplete: stereotype

The line works simultaneously as media language and software language.

03“Cab, scrubs, code — same joke, same grin”

Three occupational stereotypes appear almost like icons in a menu:

cab → taxi driver

scrubs → doctor/medical professional

code → engineer/programmer

The jobs themselves are not being mocked.

The joke is that millions of people and radically different migration histories are compressed into a handful of occupations.

“Same joke, same grin” also implicates the person performing the stereotype: the repetition is so familiar that they may regard it as harmless social shorthand.

The addendum calls these “prefab labor scripts.”

04“Whole archive in me, but you crop me to fit”

“Archive” represents the narrator's actual inheritance: Kenya, India, Britain, America, migration, empire, class, family history and paperwork.

“Crop” is visual language. An image is trimmed until it fits a predetermined frame.

But it is also a metaphor for racial classification: instead of expanding the frame to accommodate the person, the observer removes whatever does not fit the category.

That connects directly to Citizen of Empire's recurring fascination with systems that simplify humans into manageable forms.

The addendum notes that the phrase operates visually, bureaucratically and culturally at the same time.

05“Brown autocomplete every place I’ve been”

One of the song's key software metaphors.

Autocomplete predicts an entire output from a small amount of input.

The narrator supplies:

brown / Indian

The surrounding culture supplies:

job, accent, immigration status, personality, origin story.

The problem isn't simply prejudice in the traditional sense. It is premature completion: the system believes it already possesses enough information to finish the person.

This extends the album's software-logic motif, where social categories behave like badly designed interfaces.

06“your brain do sitcom math / Kwik-E-Mart voice”

“Sitcom math” describes stereotype as a crude equation.

Indian + American television = Apu/Kwik-E-Mart.

The rhyme is funny because “math” itself participates in another stereotype—the idea of South Asians as inherently technical or numerate.

So even the phrase criticizing stereotype briefly steps inside stereotypical vocabulary.

“Kwik-E-Mart voice” also points toward accent performance: racial caricature can operate through sound as much as appearance.

The line deliberately blurs professional categories.

“Dash” can evoke dashboards and tech interfaces, but also the language of app-based delivery and gig work.

The result is a deliberately jumbled employment collage: white-collar technology and service labor collapse into one brown occupational silhouette.

08“Four preset masks” / “dropdown menu”

“Preset” and “dropdown” are interface language.

A dropdown menu presents a limited set of available choices. It cannot accept complexity that its designer failed to anticipate.

That becomes the song's theory of stereotyping:

the person is not incomprehensible;

the interface has too few options.

This is closely related to the racial worksheet in “3 Migrations Deep,” where childhood identity is reduced to crude predefined categories.

10“One brown starter kit, four jobs in a jumble”

A “starter kit” is a prepackaged consumer bundle.

The lyric turns stereotype into retail packaging.

Instead of learning anything about an actual person, the observer receives a ready-made kit containing jobs, foods, accents and mannerisms.

The target of the satire is prefabrication itself.

As the original concept put it, race here works like “lazy dropdown-menu logic.”

11“Then the rent gets high and you switch up the mumble” / “First I’m “so smart,” “so driven,” “so nice”” / “Then the mood turns tight, watch the praise turn ice”

This is where the song begins changing tone.

The same immigrant who can be praised as:

  • hardworking
  • productive
  • professional

“Switch up the mumble” is important because the underlying stereotype has not disappeared.

Positive stereotype becomes negative stereotype.

That sets up the bridge's larger argument: conditional welcome depends partly on whether the immigrant is currently being imagined as economically useful.

Further artist notes

The song changed while the album changed

The early version of the concept was primarily:

America hears “Indian” → America loads stereotype.

The expanded album gave it a second question:

Why are some forms of migration considered legitimate when they arrive as economically useful labor?

That is why the finished song is harsher than its title initially suggests.

The first half attacks stereotype.

The second half attacks the economic conditions underneath the stereotype.

The newer Empire Infrastructure documentation reflects exactly that evolution, describing the track in terms of visa logic, credentialing, professional legibility and “being processed into value.”

“H-1B” is deliberately bigger than the literal visa

The song is not an autobiographical claim that the narrator's identity is an H-1B status.

H-1B functions symbolically as one of the American categories through which Indian identity becomes legible:

engineer

doctor

coder

skilled worker

visa holder

The irony is that an administrative immigration category begins acting like a racial personality type.

That is pure Citizen of Empire: paperwork escapes the form and starts telling people who they are.

The song moves from comedy to danger

The intended musical arc begins bouncy and satirical and becomes colder by Verse 3.

That production arc mirrors the lyric:

Apu → office joke → HR → visa → market → housing → polls → threat

The listener initially laughs at how stupid the stereotype is.

By the end, the song asks what happens when the exact same simplification enters politics.

The archive is deliberately oversized

Across the track:

script → caption → checkbox → file → binder → cabinet

are opposed by:

library → archive

The bureaucratic words get increasingly ridiculous because they keep failing.

The narrator's history cannot be filed correctly because the filing system assumes a simpler world than the one empire and migration actually produced.

Relationship to “3 Migrations Deep”

“H-1B-Sides” takes one problem from the opening track and zooms in.

In “3 Migrations Deep,” the narrator says “Indian” and other people translate it incorrectly.

Here we watch the translation algorithm execute.

The explicit line:

“Three migrations deep, got your frame misfiled”

turns the earlier song into evidence.

Relationship to “Protectorate Flow”

“Protectorate Flow” explains how the routes were created.

“H-1B-Sides” shows how a person produced by those routes is interpreted in the present.

That is why:

“Protectorate paperwork threaded through the fiber”

is more than a historical callback.

It says the stereotype ignores the very imperial history that made the diaspora possible.

Relationship to Panic by Design

The line:

“Same old panic with a polished spin”

quietly belongs to both albums.

Panic by Design studies how fear gets formatted into a usable product.

“H-1B-Sides” shows one political use of that mechanism: an admired worker can become a threatening migrant when economic fear needs a face.

The story changes.

The anxiety remains marketable.

The title eventually answers itself

At first, “B-side” sounds self-deprecating:

the track nobody played.

By Verse 3, it becomes a claim of depth:

“B-side, deep cut, static and hiss”

The B-side is not lesser.

It is simply the part mainstream listening habits skipped.

That makes H-1B-Sides one of the album's recurring arguments in miniature:

the problem was never that the history wasn't there.

The problem was that somebody kept playing the simplified version.
