About this song

Glass Case Alibi is a trip-hop art-pop political satire about museums, empire, cultural restitution, and the language institutions use to turn contested possession into respectable custody.

The song examines the afterlife of empire through museum collections, focusing on how objects taken during colonial expansion are reframed as preserved heritage rather than contested removals. It explores passive institutional language (“acquired,” “entered the collection”), the wealth that built many museum spaces, and the contrast between protection given to displaced artifacts versus exclusion imposed on living people in public space.

The song moves from architectural critique to historical examples such as the Benin Bronzes, Grand Tour collecting culture, and modern repatriation debates. In the bridge, the perspective shifts: the collected object speaks for itself, challenging the assumption that universal access requires permanent Western custody.

The title refers to the idea that the museum display case can function as an alibi—a mechanism that transforms questions of theft, power, and displacement into narratives of preservation, education, and public good.

Artist’s annotations

Choose an underlined lyric, or open an annotation below.

01Tobacco dressed as culture / Sugar set in stone

This refers to the colonial commodity wealth that financed many civic and cultural institutions in Britain. Cities such as Glasgow accumulated enormous fortunes through trade in tobacco and sugar produced by enslaved labor. Some museum buildings or donor fortunes are directly tied to these colonial economies.

The lyric argues that museum architecture itself can carry imperial history—not just the objects inside.

02The façade has provenance

“Provenance” usually refers to an object’s ownership history and chain of custody. The song applies the term to the museum building itself, suggesting that institutions also have origins worth scrutinizing.

This is one of the song’s central reversals: the museum is not a neutral container for history—it is part of the historical evidence.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
03Antiques Roadshow / No one asks who built the road

This references Antiques Roadshow, a television program where experts assess objects’ value and provenance.

The lyric extends “provenance” beyond ownership into infrastructure and empire. The “road” symbolizes colonial trade routes, shipping lanes, and extraction systems that enabled objects to move into Western collections.

The joke asks: when valuing an artifact, who examines the systems that moved it?

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
04They took the verb out of the sentence / Put the evidence in glass

Museums often use passive language such as “acquired,” “entered the collection,” or “obtained,” which obscures who acted and under what conditions.

Removing the verb removes agency and accountability.

The “glass” refers to museum display cases, which preserve and legitimize the object while distancing viewers from the contested circumstances of its removal.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
05Indy said adventure / Britain heard Bloomsbury

“Indy” refers to Indiana Jones, famous for saying artifacts “belong in a museum.”

“Bloomsbury” refers to the London district containing the British Museum.

The line asks whose museum is assumed to be the natural home of cultural heritage. It critiques the implicit idea that “museum” often really means a major Western institution.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
06Benin cast the history / Britain cast the claim

This refers to the Benin Bronzes, thousands of royal and ceremonial works looted during the British punitive expedition against Benin City in 1897.

The word “cast” works as a double meaning:

  • bronze casting as artistic process
  • casting as shaping a narrative or interpretation

Benin created the objects; Britain shaped the story around them.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
07Safety is not ownership / And care cannot confer a claim

A common defense against repatriation is that Western museums preserve artifacts safely and make them accessible to global audiences.

The song argues that preservation and care do not automatically create moral ownership. Security and conservation are not equivalent to consent or rightful possession.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
08One theft gets a glass case / Another gets a charge

This is one of the song’s thesis lines.

It contrasts two kinds of displacement:

  • colonial acquisition of cultural objects, legitimized by institutions
  • criminalized presence of marginalized people, especially the homeless

The lyric points to unequal treatment of theft, possession, and occupation depending on power.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
09Grand Tour rules: if it fits, call it souvenir

The Grand Tour was a traditional educational journey through Europe undertaken by wealthy elites, especially British aristocrats.

Travelers often collected antiquities, art, and fragments of classical sites. The lyric satirizes this as respectable souvenir theft.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
10Byron’s name upon the temple

This refers to the Temple of Poseidon at Cape Sounion in Greece, where “Byron” is carved into stone.

It is commonly associated with Lord Byron, though historians debate whether he personally made the inscription.

The lyric highlights how aristocratic vandalism can become romanticized as heritage over time.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
11Hostile architecture

Hostile architecture refers to design features that discourage behaviors such as sleeping, resting, or gathering in public spaces—examples include anti-homeless spikes, segmented benches, and armrests designed to prevent lying down.

The song links this to the album’s broader themes of criminalized presence and exclusion.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
12Some rooms name the elephant

This references museums that openly address colonial histories and contested acquisitions.

The phrase also alludes to Birmingham Museum’s exhibition “The Elephant in the Room,” which explores empire, provenance, repatriation, and museum ethics.

The song acknowledges that transparency is meaningful—but argues that acknowledgment alone does not resolve ownership.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
13Too sacred to exhibit / Still retained all the same

Some sacred objects, including Ethiopian tabots held in British collections, are considered too sacred for public display or ordinary study.

The lyric highlights the contradiction of retaining sacred objects while acknowledging their spiritual significance and restricted visibility.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
14Loaned back to a homeland

Some disputed cultural objects are returned only as loans, with legal ownership remaining with the lending institution.

The lyric critiques the irony of a country “borrowing back” objects central to its own heritage.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
15I was not discovered / I was already known

This is the bridge’s perspective shift: the object speaks.

The line challenges colonial narratives of “discovery,” pointing out that many places, objects, and cultures labeled as “discovered” were already known and meaningful to the people who created them.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
16Your record starts in London / My history starts at home

Museum records often begin at accession—the point when the institution acquires an object.

The object reminds the listener that institutional documentation captures only a small fragment of its existence.

Its real history begins long before museum custody.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
17The evidence is still there

The final line returns to the title’s core metaphor.

The display case functions as an institutional alibi—but it also preserves the evidence.

The museum’s attempt to legitimize possession unintentionally keeps the proof of displacement visible to anyone willing to read the record carefully.
