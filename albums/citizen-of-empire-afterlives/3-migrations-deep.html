About this song

“3 Migrations Deep” is the opening thesis statement of Citizen of Empire: a self-introduction that refuses the childish Black-or-white binary and answers it with a migration history instead. The song begins in a classroom, where the narrator is asked to compress herself into a simple racial category, then expands outward into a longer story shaped by Indian ancestry, East African birthright, British imperial residue, U.S. upbringing, and a later return to the UK.

The title is a clarified evolution of the earlier working identity “A-Thrice,” itself a play on “A-twice migrant.” In the family history behind the song, the parents’ East African Asian route to Britain already fits the language of repeated migration; the later move onward to the United States and the narrator’s eventual migration back to the UK makes the family story “three migrations deep.” The song therefore rejects the idea that the speaker is hard to understand. What actually fails is the racial and national shorthand used to read her.

The chorus also turns one of the earliest wounds into one of the sharpest replies. “What tribe?” begins as the kind of ignorant American question that mistakes “Indian” for “Native American” and assumes any complicated identity must fit a documentary stereotype. By the hook, that same question is answered with “Forty-fourth,” invoking the recognition of Kenyans of Asian origin as Kenya’s 44th tribe and transforming a schoolyard misreading into a historical correction.

Artist’s annotations

Choose an underlined lyric, or open an annotation below.

01“a color”

Literal meaning: The classroom wants a simple racial answer—Black or white—rather than a history.

Wordplay / double meaning: “Color” sounds elementary and visual, which is the point: the lyric criticizes a way of seeing identity that reduces it to surface.

Why it matters: This phrase establishes the whole song’s conflict. The speaker is being asked for pigment when the real answer is route, empire, and migration.

Connection to album / canon: Citizen of Empire repeatedly shows ordinary introductions becoming political because imperial categories still organize the present.

02“I came from an empire.”

The reply expands a classroom demand for a racial category into a history of empire and migration. Identity is explained through routes and inherited systems rather than reduced to appearance.

03“crayon pack”

Literal meaning: The speaker is reduced to a childish color chart.

Wordplay / image: A crayon box suggests primary colors, a classroom setting, and a crude visual taxonomy. It mocks the smallness of the framework being imposed on her.

Why it matters: The metaphor keeps the scene in childhood while exposing how early racial sorting begins.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
04“Columbus named the frame”

Literal meaning: The question “What tribe?” comes out of the old American misuse of “Indian,” rooted in Columbus reaching the Americas while searching for India.

Layered meaning: “Frame” matters as much as “named.” The line is about the inherited conceptual frame that mislabels people before they even speak.

Why it matters: This is where the song links a schoolroom question to a five-century colonial error.

Artist-intent context: In drafting, the song explicitly connected the childhood “what tribe?” question to Americans assuming “Indian” meant Native American.

05“masquerade”

Literal meaning: The speaker says one word—“Indian”—and it comes back wearing someone else’s meaning.

Wordplay: A masquerade is costume, performance, and mistaken identity all at once.

Why it matters: The line captures the recurring violence of misnaming. Her self-description is returned to her in disguise.

Connection to album / canon: A-Thrice songs often show language itself as infrastructure: labels do not simply describe people, they route them.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
06“Too much history for your eyesight”

Literal meaning: The listener’s visual categories cannot hold the speaker’s actual history.

Layered meaning: “Eyesight” suggests both literal sight and social legibility. The critique is not only of prejudice but of a weak perceptual system.

Why it matters: This line turns the problem around. The speaker is not unintelligible; the viewer’s framework is underpowered.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
07“A-Thrice” / “Three migrations deep”

Literal meaning: The refrain explains the persona through family movement across multiple migrations.

Wordplay / backstory: “A-Thrice” grows out of the phrase “A-twice migrant.” In the writing history, the name becomes sharper once the family route is understood as being moved again and then moved back.

Why it matters: The rename to “3 Migrations Deep” makes explicit what the original persona name implied: the self is not “mixed up,” but historically layered.

Connection to album / canon: This is the album’s opening act of self-definition.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
08“Forty-fourth”

Literal meaning: The answer points to the recognition of Kenyans of Asian origin as Kenya’s 44th tribe.

Layered meaning: The line flips a rude question into an archival comeback. It works as both punchline and documentation.

Why it matters: This is one of the song’s most satisfying reversals: the child who once had no answer now answers with history.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
09“H-1B”

The lyric is satirizing a narrow U.S. stereotype: that ‘Indian’ must mean recent immigrant, tech worker, visa line, and corporate lanyard. The H-1B visa is a U.S. temporary work visa for specialty occupations, and it became shorthand in American culture for one narrow story about South Asian migration. The song rejects that reduction. It also matters that the narrator’s family reached the U.S. through British migration routes rather than the pathways many Indian nationals had to navigate under U.S. immigration caps and employment sponsorship systems.

10“India in the bloodline” / “Africa in the backstory” / “U.S. in the upbringing”

These lines map the route in compressed form. ‘Bloodline,’ ‘backstory,’ and ‘upbringing’ sound almost like metadata fields, which matches the song’s habit of using bureaucratic language to tell intimate history.

11“British shadows stitched across the text”

Literal meaning: Empire shows up in documents, classifications, and inherited records.

Wordplay: “Text” can mean both literal paperwork and the larger text of history. “Stitched” suggests something sewn into the fabric rather than openly announced.

Why it matters: The line names the imperial afterlife directly. Britain is not only a place in the route; it is an organizing structure inside the archive.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
12“white in how that worked”

This line points to the social coding of Britishness. Outside the UK — and often even inside it — people still collapse ‘English’ or ‘British’ into ‘white,’ despite the long multicultural reality of London and the UK. The line says the paperwork may say UK, but social reading still withholds full British legibility from nonwhite bodies.

13“Twice migrants”

This is the song’s hidden engine. The phrase names a repeated-migration pattern common to many East African Asian families whose routes ran through empire: Indian ancestry, East African birth, British connection, then onward movement again. The song expands that pattern into a generational self-definition.

14“mah-reh-KAH-nee”

Swahili for ‘America.’ Its appearance matters because the family route is not only Indian/British; East African language and social texture are also part of the speaker’s inheritance.

15“Home sounded normal”

Home feels ordinary to the narrator even when others read it as foreign. The contrast holds together a familiar private world and the externally imposed status of outsider.

16“Hoo jeh choon teh j choon”

The Gujarati declaration હું જે છું તે જ છું means “I am who I am.” The performed pronunciation carries the answer into the voice itself. It responds to the pressure to simplify: the statement is declarative rather than defensive.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
17“par-day-shee”

Pardeshi, performed here as “par-day-shee,” is Gujarati for “foreigner” or “outsider.” The line sets an ordinary home against a stranger’s reading of the narrator, carrying the song’s larger pressure of being assigned an elsewhere.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
18“U.K. in the paperwork, not white in how that worked”

The song’s migration logic sits inside British imperial history. South Asian movement to East Africa and later movement to Britain did not happen outside empire; it followed imperial trade, labor, and legal routes. That is why the song keeps insisting that what looks contradictory to an outsider is, historically, entirely coherent.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
19“I came from an empire”

Literal meaning: The answer to the classroom question is not a neat ethnic label but a political history.

Layered meaning: The line scales up immediately from child-speech to imperial aftermath. It also reframes “Where are you from?” as a question with structural, not merely geographic, answers.

Why it matters: This is the song’s thesis in miniature: personal identity is inseparable from the systems that produced the route.

Connection to album / canon: The album’s core argument is that formal empire ended, but its borders, paperwork, and social categories remained.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
20“Born in Kenya, British shadows stitched across the text”

The line locates the parents’ birthplace in Kenya while the chorus names India as ancestry and Africa as backstory. Family birthplace, ancestry, nationality and upbringing occupy different fields; the song refuses to flatten them into a single answer to where someone is “from.”

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
21“Africa in the backstory” / “U.S. in the upbringing”

Africa names the family’s East African history; the U.S. names the narrator’s upbringing. The hook treats identity as a layered route rather than a single birthplace or administrative category.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
22“They asked for one truth. / I gave them something higher.”

Literal meaning: The closing idea rejects the demand for a single clean answer.

Layered meaning: “Higher” does not mean vaguer. It means more structurally true. The answer moves above stereotype and toward history.

Why it matters: The final gesture refuses reduction without becoming defensive. It closes the song by replacing the demand for one label with a fuller account of how the self was made.

Connection to album / canon: The album repeatedly argues that oversimplified labels are not neutral; they are products of empire, bureaucracy, and public narration.

Language and context
  • Marekani: Swahili: America / the United States.
  • kawaida: Swahili: normal, usual, ordinary.
  • Hoon je chhu, te j chhu: Anglicized Gujarati: “I am who I am.”
  • pardeshi: Gujarati: foreigner, outsider, someone from another land.
  • H-1B: A U.S. temporary work visa for specialty occupations; used here as shorthand for a narrow American stereotype about Indian migration.
