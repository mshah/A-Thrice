About this song

“Made in London” is the emotional answer to the question that runs underneath Citizen of Empire: Afterlives: what grows after empire?

The album spends much of its running time examining the systems empire left behind—borders, paperwork, racial categories, routes, punishment, museums, labor markets and inherited infrastructure. “Empire Architecture” argues that those systems can be rewritten. “Made in London” takes the argument out of the abstract and into a city where some of that rewriting has already happened through ordinary human life. The old routes have become neighborhoods, accents, music, food, friendships and crowds. The former imperial capital has itself been changed by the people who arrived through the histories Britain helped create.

But the rewritten song makes that political idea personal. Earlier Citizen of Empire songs repeatedly force the narrator to have an answer ready: What are you? What tribe? Where are you really from? Which box? Which route? In “Made in London,” the breakthrough is almost anticlimactic: nobody asks. The narrator can ride the train, walk through a neighborhood, hear multiple accents and simply exist without presenting a family tree as evidence.

That is why the chorus works in both directions. London has been “made” by generations of migration, but the narrator has also been made in London—not in the sense of ancestry or birthplace, but through experience, belonging, culture and the freedom to become a whole person without constant explanation.

Musically, the song performs the same argument. Its core is bright 90s-inspired funk-rock/pop, while dhol, Indian melodic ornament, Caribbean guitar rhythm and dancehall-informed low end are integrated into the same band rather than presented as separate cultural showcases. The arrangement treats mixture as normal musical grammar, mirroring the London the lyrics celebrate.

Artist’s annotations

Choose an underlined lyric, or open an annotation below.

01“Kilburn let me walk in whole, Camden never asked me why”

“Walk in whole” is the emotional opposite of much of Citizen of Empire. Earlier songs repeatedly divide the narrator into components—Indian, African, British, American—and ask which part explains her properly. Here, the city does not require that disassembly.

Kilburn and Camden matter because they are not being used as tourist landmarks. They are ordinary lived London. The freedom being celebrated is correspondingly ordinary: walking through a neighborhood without having to turn yourself into an explanation.

The phrase “never asked me why” quietly answers an album full of questions.

02“West Hampstead in the morning, just another face passing by”

For much of the album, being noticed means being classified.

Here, anonymity becomes belonging.

“Just another face” might sound dismissive elsewhere, but within this album it is relief. The narrator is no longer the unusual person in the room whose presence demands an origin story. She is simply another Londoner going somewhere in the morning.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
03“Train doors open, city moving, nobody checking where I fit”

Transit imagery runs throughout Afterlives: ships, ports, borders, customs systems and migration routes repeatedly determine who is allowed to move.

The train reverses that logic. The doors simply open.

“Checking where I fit” also echoes the forms, checkboxes and classifications of “3 Migrations Deep,” “What Tribe?,” “H-1B-Sides” and “Reply All.” The same project that began with identity functioning like an inspection ends with movement that requires no identity test.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
04“All those answers at the ready — turns out I didn’t need them”

This is one of the song’s major emotional pivots.

Across Citizen of Empire, the narrator has learned to keep an explanation ready because seemingly casual encounters can become interrogations: ancestry, nationality, race, migration history and family route all become evidence.

London’s gift in this song is not a better question.

It is the absence of the question.

The casual “turns out” is important too. After an album of elaborate historical answers, one of its most meaningful discoveries arrives almost conversationally: she did all that preparation and nobody demanded the presentation.

05“All my life I kept an answer ready / Name, route, reason, family tree”

This pre-chorus compresses the entire original Citizen of Empire identity problem into four fields.

Name recalls misnaming and surname history.

Route recalls migration and empire.

Reason suggests having to justify why someone is here.

Family tree turns ancestry into supporting documentation.

The vocabulary resembles an immigration form or interrogation even though the scene is social. That is intentional: earlier songs repeatedly show bureaucratic logic leaking into ordinary questions about identity.

The payoff is the next line:

“Nobody asked me who to be.”

The narrator has moved from being asked what she is to being free simply to be.

06“Then London let me just exist — and somehow that felt new”

This is arguably the emotional thesis of the rewritten song.

The album has already established that misrecognition is exhausting not only because people get the facts wrong, but because the narrator is repeatedly forced to perform correction.

“Just exist” therefore becomes radical in its simplicity.

The line also avoids claiming that London has perfectly solved race, class or belonging. The feeling described is personal and experiential: in this city, the narrator discovered spaces where identity could become ordinary rather than evidentiary.

07“Made in London”

The title has several simultaneous meanings.

First, it borrows the language of a manufacturing label: Made in London.

Second, it describes cultural formation rather than ancestry. The speaker may carry histories from elsewhere, but London helped form the person singing the song.

Third, it refers to London itself. Modern London has also been “made” by generations of migration, labor, music, food and cultural exchange.

The chorus therefore sets up reciprocal authorship:

“We made the place”

“And the city made me too”

The migrant changes the city; the city changes the migrant.

08“Every tongue in the bassline”

“Tongue” means language, accent and voice, while “bassline” gives those differences a shared rhythmic foundation.

The lyric does not imagine multiculturalism as everyone eventually sounding the same. The languages remain distinct; what changes is that they can occupy the same song.

That matters for Afterlives, whose final answer is not cultural purity or assimilation but overlapping culture.

The production reinforces the lyric by putting Indian, Caribbean and funk-rock elements into the same groove.

09“You drew the map / We made the place”

This is the song’s most compressed distinction between designed systems and lived reality.

“They” drew borders, routes and administrative geographies.

“We” made neighborhoods, culture, relationships and everyday life inside and across them.

A map can tell people where the lines are supposed to be. It cannot determine what eventually grows along those lines.

The couplet also directly answers the map imagery running throughout Citizen of Empire. Earlier maps misread the narrator. Here, lived culture exceeds the map altogether.

10“And the city made me too”

This prevents “We made the place” from becoming a simple reversal of ownership.

The narrator is not claiming that migrants conquered London back or that the city belongs exclusively to a new group. The relationship is reciprocal.

People changed London.

London also changed them.

That is why “Made in London” becomes a belonging statement rather than a territorial one.

11“Jerk smoke, masala, same street air” / “Dhol riding dancehall low end”

The first line puts Caribbean and South Asian sensory references into literally the same air.

The second makes the combination musical. Dhol evokes South Asian percussion; dancehall brings Jamaican and Caribbean rhythmic lineage. Neither has to disappear for the groove to work.

This is also reflected in the track’s production design, which deliberately integrates dhol and Indian melodic details with Caribbean guitar and dancehall-informed bass inside a funk-rock/pop band rather than dividing them into separate “ethnic” sections.

The music therefore becomes evidence for the lyric’s argument.

12“pirate echoes turned to gold”

“Pirate” points toward London’s tradition of pirate radio: unofficial stations and broadcasts that helped music circulate outside established commercial channels.

“Turned to gold” can be read several ways:

  • underground sound becoming mainstream culture
  • marginalized scenes becoming commercially valuable
  • music once treated as peripheral becoming part of London's cultural identity

The line fits the song’s larger transformation motif: what begins outside official recognition can eventually become central to the city’s sound.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
13“Every crossing sounded local”

“Crossing” works simultaneously as migration and musical crossover.

The paradox is the point: something does not have to be culturally pure to become local.

London’s local sound is partly the sound of things crossing—people, languages, rhythms, food, accents and genres.

That turns one of the album’s oldest anxieties on its head. Earlier songs ask where something originally came from. “Made in London” asks what happens after different histories have lived together long enough to create something new.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
14“Different histories, one crowd — nobody asking which is mine”

This is another direct resolution of the album’s identity interrogation.

The histories have not disappeared. The narrator does not have to erase difference to belong.

What disappears is the demand to choose and display the correct one.

The “one crowd” therefore represents coexistence rather than sameness: multiple histories remain present without every individual being required to account for hers.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
15“Empire drew the routes between us, thought those lines would carry through / We took everything that crossed them — made the old map something new”

This couplet compresses the central historical argument of Afterlives.

Empire built routes for its own purposes: administration, trade, labor, extraction and control. Those routes did not disappear when formal imperial rule ended. People continued moving along and through them.

But the consequences exceeded the original design.

Migration produced families, neighborhoods, cuisines, accents, genres and publics that imperial planners did not control.

“Made the old map something new” therefore does not deny the violent history of the routes. It describes what people created after inheriting them.

This is the cultural counterpart to “Empire Architecture,” whose thesis is that inherited routes do not obligate later generations to preserve the original code.

16“They drew the lines, we crossed them / They named the world, we changed it”

The bridge deliberately returns to the language of colonial mapping and classification.

Drawing and naming are acts of authority: deciding where borders go and what places or peoples will be called.

Crossing and changing are acts that exceed that authority.

The lyric is not claiming that postcolonial migration erases colonial history. It argues that the classifications imposed by power cannot permanently determine what later generations make from the world they inherit.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
17“Every route they thought they owned / Came home sounding different”

“Came home” contains a deliberate irony.

The routes of empire once radiated outward from the imperial center. Migration later moved people, languages and cultures along those connections toward Britain itself.

When those routes “come home,” they do not return unchanged. They return as accents, music, neighborhoods, food and new forms of Britishness.

“Sounding different” makes that imperial return audible.

It also leads directly into:

“The future has an accent.”

18“The future has an accent / You can hear it everywhere”

Accent has often functioned elsewhere in the A-Thrice catalog as evidence used to place someone: Where are you from? What are you? Which identity does that voice belong to?

Here accent stops being evidence of foreignness and becomes evidence of the future.

There is no single neutral voice toward which everyone else is gradually converging. The city’s multiplicity is itself what comes next.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
19“Empire wanted subjects / It accidentally made neighbours”

This is the historical punchline of the song and one of the clearest statements of the expanded album’s thesis.

Empire wanted subjects: populations governed, administered, taxed, moved or incorporated into imperial systems.

But empire also created connections—routes, legal relationships, languages, institutions and migration pathways—that survived formal imperial rule.

Later generations used those connections in ways the imperial system did not control, including migration to Britain itself.

“Accidentally” is therefore ironic. The line does not suggest empire intended multicultural harmony or that its violence is redeemed by later diversity. It points to an unintended historical consequence: a system designed around hierarchy helped create the conditions for people from across that system eventually to become literal neighbors.

The movement from subject → neighbour is also the album’s movement from imperial classification toward shared civic life.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
20“Southbank light / Train home / Bass through brick”

After the bridge expands into empire, maps and historical consequence, the outro deliberately comes back down to ordinary city life.

Light.

A ride home.

Music coming through a wall.

That scale change matters. The album does not conclude with an abstract political program. Its final evidence is lived experience: people sharing infrastructure and culture in a city that has already become more complicated than the systems that once tried to organize them.

“Bass through brick” is especially fitting as the final image before the title returns. Borders are solid lines.

Sound travels through walls.

Further artist notes

The new version changes the song’s center of gravity

The earlier “Made in London” leaned more heavily on describing London: places, arts institutions, neighborhoods and multicultural public culture.

The rewrite asks a more emotional question:

What did London feel like to the person who spent the rest of the album explaining herself?

Its answer is:

“Turns out I didn’t need them.”

That makes this version much more directly connected to the opening of the album.

The album begins with interrogation and ends with nobody asking

At the beginning of Citizen of Empire, a child is asked:

What are you?

Then:

What tribe?

Across the album those questions mutate into forms, borders, files, visas, archives and political arguments.

The final song does not produce the ultimate answer.

It reaches a place where the answer is no longer demanded.

That is why:

“Nobody asked me who to be”

is one of the most important resolution lines in the entire project.

“Made in London” and “British Asian Standard Time”

“British Asian Standard Time” describes the relief of entering a cultural environment where jokes, family codes and references are already legible.

“Made in London” expands that idea from community recognition to civic belonging.

The narrator is no longer simply in a room where her particular background makes sense.

She is in a city where multiple backgrounds can sound ordinary at once.

“Made in London” and “Protectorate Flow”

“Protectorate Flow” asks listeners not to act surprised that diasporas exist when empire built the routes.

“Made in London” shows what happened generations later.

The route that once carried imperial commerce and administration has become a route along which people, food, accents and music moved.

The earlier track diagnoses the infrastructure.

The closer listens to what grew from it.

“Made in London” and “Reply All”

“Reply All” is the moment the narrator finally sends the complete explanation:

every wrong question gets the whole thread back.

“Made in London” goes one step further.

There is no thread to answer.

The move from “I finally have the complete answer” to “I don't always need to provide an answer” is an important difference between self-definition and belonging.

“Made in London” and “Empire Architecture”

These songs give Afterlives its two-part ending.

“Empire Architecture” says:

Inherited systems can be rewritten.

“Made in London” says:

Look around. People have already been rewriting them culturally.

The first song thinks in code, permissions, routes and architecture.

The second thinks in accents, basslines, streets and crowds.

The concept document explicitly pairs them as the album’s two forms of resolution.

The production is part of the thesis

The song is not structured as:

rock section → Indian section → Caribbean section.

Instead, those musical languages coexist inside the same arrangement.

A funk-rock bassline can sit beneath dhol.

Caribbean guitar rhythm can coexist with an Indian melodic ornament.

That musical choice reflects the lyric:

“Every crossing sounded local.”

The cultures do not have to remain in separate display cases in order to remain recognizable.

They can make a new local sound together.

The final transformation

Across Afterlives, routes repeatedly mean:

empire

migration

trade

border

permission

classification

At the very end, route becomes:

the train home.

That may be the quietest structural resolution in the song.

The route is no longer something the narrator has to explain as evidence of why she exists.

It is simply how she gets home.
