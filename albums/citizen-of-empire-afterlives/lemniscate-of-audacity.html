About this song

“Lemniscate of Audacity” is A-Thrice’s mathematical diss track: a deliberately excessive, Mean Girls-adjacent bonus-song flex that turns the language of calculus, topology, probability, and proof into a vocabulary for humiliation. Instead of insulting its target with generic diss-rap clichés, the song treats arrogance like a badly constructed theorem. Every boast becomes something to test, differentiate, reduce, or disprove.

Because it sits outside the main political arc of Citizen of Empire, the track works as an encore rather than a chapter in the album’s imperial argument. The main record ends by reclaiming the right to self-definition; this bonus track then releases that tension through play, virtuosity, and verbal overkill. The persona here is not explaining history to the listener. She is enjoying mastery — of rhythm, of references, and of a language usually treated as cold or inaccessible.

The song’s classroom frame — “Class in session,” “Show your work,” “No curve,” “No extra credit” — matters almost as much as the equations. It makes the diss feel like an exam the target cannot pass. Mathematics becomes the opposite of hand-waving: a symbolic way of saying that confidence is not proof, swagger is not substance, and repetition does not make a claim true. The target of satire is not math itself, but a very familiar type of overconfident boy who thinks charm, volume, and entitlement can substitute for rigor.

At the same time, the track is generous to listeners who do not know the math. Most of the terms still work at the level of attitude: a “rounding error” is someone reduced in importance; “undefined” is someone who collapses under pressure; a “lemniscate” is an infinity-shaped loop, so a “lemniscate of audacity” means endless nerve. That double structure is what makes the song work so well. It is a battle rap, a joke machine, and a mini-lesson in how technical language can be turned into theater.

Artist’s annotations

01“lemniscate of audacity”

A lemniscate is a figure-eight curve, visually similar to the infinity symbol. In plain English, the line says this person’s nerve is endless. A-Thrice chooses the term because it sounds more precise and more ridiculous than simply saying “infinite audacity,” which fits the song’s whole method: using technical language to make the target sound both overblown and mathematically trapped in the same loop.

02“confidence interval”

In statistics, a confidence interval is a range used to estimate where a true value is likely to fall. The joke is that the target has lots of ‘confidence’ in the everyday sense, but no evidentiary basis in the statistical sense. The line turns swagger into bad data.

03“real limit”

A limit describes what a function approaches as its input gets close to some value. Here it means that whenever reality actually presses in — the ‘real limit’ — the target breaks down. The bar works even for non-math listeners because ‘limit’ also means the point where someone’s act stops working.

04“Topologically loud”

Topology studies properties that survive stretching and bending without tearing. Saying someone is ‘topologically loud’ means their shape of behavior stays obnoxious no matter how it is dressed up or remapped. The follow-up — ‘but your substance collapse’ — says the volume survives, not the content.

05“one-line contradiction”

A contradiction is a statement that defeats itself. ‘One-line’ makes it feel cheap and immediate: the target does not even need a full argument to fall apart. The phrase also echoes mathematical proof language, where a contradiction can end a proof by showing an assumption cannot hold.

06“Mean Girls energy”

This names the song’s tone openly: catty, schoolish, highly social, and deliberately theatrical. The diss is savage, but it is not grim. A-Thrice wanted the track to feel like it could sit somewhere between battle rap and the social cruelty of a teen comedy.

07“not proven”

In mathematics, a theorem only counts once it is proven. To ‘walk in like a theorem’ but leave ‘not proven’ means arriving with authority and leaving exposed as unsupported. It is one of the clearest statements of the song’s larger logic: assertion is not evidence.

08“rounding error”

A rounding error is a small inaccuracy produced when numbers are simplified. As an insult, it means someone is not even the main calculation — just an annoying leftover discrepancy. The line is funny because it reduces the target from ‘big ego’ to tiny numerical noise.

09“piecewise smooth”

A piecewise function is defined by different rules on different intervals. ‘Piecewise smooth’ suggests the target can appear polished in segments — maybe in one room, one context, one performance — but lacks real consistency across the whole domain. The lie looks smooth only in pieces.

10“take derivative”

A derivative measures rate of change: how something moves, bends, or shifts. In diss-track terms, once the speaker applies scrutiny — once she ‘takes derivative’ — the target’s fake stability disappears and their underlying behavior becomes visible. The line turns analysis into an act of exposure.

11“slope says flat / Second derivative ugly”

The slope is the first derivative, telling you whether something is rising or falling; the second derivative tells you about curvature. A-Thrice uses both like a forensic report. ‘Slope says flat’ means no real upward motion. ‘Second derivative ugly’ adds a body joke while also saying the deeper shape is bad, not just the surface trend.

12“Function with a hole”

A function with a hole has a point where the graph is missing even though the curve may appear otherwise intact. Calling someone ‘a function with a hole where the truth should be’ means the missing part is structural, not accidental: the exact place where coherence should exist is empty.

13“All domain in public, no range privately”

The domain is the set of allowed inputs; the range is the set of outputs a function can produce. ‘All domain in public, no range privately’ means the target shows up everywhere, takes up space, and performs publicly, but produces very little of value in intimate or real-life settings.

14“Show your work”

This is both classroom language and a moral demand. In school, showing your work proves you did not just guess. In the song, it becomes the central indictment: the target wants full credit for claims they cannot substantiate.

15“Bernoulli trial with one outcome: miss”

A Bernoulli trial is a simple probability experiment with only two outcomes, usually framed as success or failure. The joke is that every trial produces the same result here: failure. It is a compact way of calling the target reliably, boringly wrong.

16“Bernoulli’s lemniscate”

The lemniscate of Bernoulli is a specific figure-eight curve in geometry. The song doubles down on the opener by saying the target’s so-called ‘infinite potential’ is just another loop — repeated, circular, and going nowhere. It sounds grand; the point is that the pattern is stale.

17“overfit”

In statistics and machine learning, overfitting happens when a model clings too tightly to a tiny training set and then performs badly in the real world. As an insult, it means the target learned the wrong lesson from one compliment and built their whole personality around it.

18“prove contradiction”

Proof by contradiction is a classic method: assume something is true, then show that assumption leads to absurdity. ‘Whole friend group prove contradiction on your ass’ means the target’s own social history has already tested and refuted their self-image.

19“Imaginary swagger, no real part inside”

Complex numbers have a real part and an imaginary part. The line says the swagger is all imaginary component with no real substance. It is one of the song’s cleanest math jokes because even listeners who have forgotten the formal definition can hear the contrast between ‘imaginary’ and ‘real.’

20“straight little line in a curved-space event”

This line mocks fake claims to depth or multidimensionality. In geometry and physics, curved spaces change how lines behave. Calling someone a ‘straight little line’ inside a curved-space event means they are comically simplistic inside a setting that actually requires complexity.

21“mug, donut, same class”

This is a famous topology joke: a coffee mug and a donut are considered equivalent because each has one hole and can be deformed into the other without tearing. A-Thrice uses that well-known example to say the target and a red flag are structurally the same thing. Different surface, same underlying form.

22“manifold”

A manifold is a space that may be complicated overall but looks ordinary in small local neighborhoods. Here it is used mockingly: the target recycles the same game across situations, pretending depth while actually repeating a familiar pattern.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
23“Undefined”

Some expressions in math are undefined because the operation simply does not produce a valid value. In the bridge, ‘Undefined when the blame comes near’ means the target loses shape and meaning the minute accountability enters the room.

24“Asymptote courage”

An asymptote is a line a curve approaches but never reaches. ‘Asymptote courage’ means bravery that always gets close in theory but never actually arrives when needed. It is cowardice dressed up as almost-action.

25“the set looks dense”

In topology and analysis, a dense set is one that lies arbitrarily close to every point in a space. Here it means the target is not unique at all. This type of behavior is everywhere; basic boy behavior fills the space.

26“Non-Euclidean ethics”

Euclidean geometry runs on stable axioms. Non-Euclidean geometry changes the rules. Calling someone’s ethics ‘non-Euclidean’ jokes that their moral system bends depending on applause, convenience, or social reward. The problem is not complexity; it is inconsistency.

27“limit from the left and right”

In calculus, you often check the left-hand limit and right-hand limit to see whether a function truly approaches the same value from both sides. ‘Our patience got a limit from the left and right’ means there is no ambiguity here: no matter how you approach the situation, patience is ending.

28“Thought you were a series, elegant sum” / “You diverge on contact, nothing stable will come”

A series is a sum of terms, and convergence means it approaches a stable value; divergence means it does not settle. The target imagines themselves as some elegant, accumulating whole, but in practice they ‘diverge on contact’ — nothing stable results once real life is involved.

29“Finite tantrum yelling ‘I’m infinite’”

This is the song’s thesis in miniature. The target wants infinity-symbol scale — endless specialness, endless importance — but is actually small, temporary, and childish. The mismatch between self-image and actual magnitude is the diss.

30“Cancel common factors”

When simplifying fractions, you cancel factors that appear in both numerator and denominator. The lyric imagines reducing the target down to the smallest honest form. What survives after all the inflation is stripped away is not greatness but emptiness.

31“One over ego with a zero underneath”

If zero sits in the denominator, the expression is undefined; division by zero breaks ordinary arithmetic. So the final insult is mathematically impossible on purpose. The target’s self-construction is not just small or incorrect — it is structurally incoherent.

32“No curve / No extra credit”

The outro returns to school language. A grading curve or extra credit can rescue weak work. The speaker denies both. In narrative terms, the target does not get mercy, reinterpretation, or post hoc padding. The verdict stands.

Further artist notes
  • Target of satire: not mathematics, and not ‘nerdiness’ in general, but boys whose confidence outruns their substance. Math is the forensic instrument, not the victim.
  • The song’s hidden structure is evaluative. It begins like a classroom, moves through proof language, then ends with grading language. That arc makes the whole diss feel like an exam the target cannot pass.
  • Within the larger Citizen of Empire project, the track functions as a pressure-release encore. After the heavy political and autobiographical work of the main album, this bonus cut showcases another A-Thrice strength: technical verbal play for its own sake.
  • Even here, the song quietly echoes a larger album habit: mistrusting unsupported claims. The main record questions national myths, bureaucratic categories, and official narratives; this bonus track miniaturizes that skepticism into interpersonal form. Show your work, or the claim does not stand.
