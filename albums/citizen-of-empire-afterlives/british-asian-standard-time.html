About this song

“British Asian Standard Time” is the warm, funny, socially specific release valve in Citizen of Empire. After the album’s opening songs deal with interrogation, misrecognition, and historical correction, this track moves into a different emotional register: the pleasure of being legible inside a culture whose timing, jokes, foods, kinship terms, weddings, and class cues make immediate sense. Its central contrast is semantic as much as geographic. In Britain, “Asian” often lands with South Asian specificity; in the United States, the same word is more likely to default elsewhere, forcing the speaker into footnotes. The song turns that gap into comedy and comfort, moving from representational jokes about Ilford and EastEnders to domestic details like proper chai, mogo chips, sari reties, and auntie surveillance. The hook’s shifting phone-call relatives — masi, faeba, bhen — make the track feel lived-in and diasporic at once, sketching a family network that is large, close, and constantly in touch across countries. Musically, the song is designed to feel upbeat and communal, almost wedding-adjacent: playful drums, bounce, groove, and inside jokes arriving at the speed of recognition.

01“British Asian Standard Time”

The title works as both a cultural timezone and a social operating system. It jokes on “standard time” as something measurable and ordinary, while arguing that British Asian life has its own inherited rhythm: jokes land faster, references need less explanation, and certain rooms become instantly legible. The phrase also reframes identity as tempo rather than blood purity — not a static essence, but a shared timing.

02“Late for your stereotype”

This flips an old racist cliché into a timing joke. The line says the speaker may be “late” only relative to someone else’s lazy script, while remaining perfectly on time for her own life. It is a concise statement of the whole track’s politics: misrecognition is the other person’s scheduling error.

03“mah-si”

Gujarati for maternal aunt. In the first hook, the word grounds the song in family intimacy rather than abstraction: the speaker is not just discussing diaspora in theory, she is already mid-conversation with it.

04“fay-ba”

Gujarati kinship term for paternal aunt. The switch from masi to faeba in a later hook matters: the song keeps changing which female relative is “on the line,” suggesting a big, close family network spread across places but held together through calls, messages, and constant contact.

05“bhen”

Gujarati for sister. By the final hook, the phone line moves again from an aunt to a sibling generation. That progression keeps the hook alive while also showing how British Asian family life often means multiple overlapping circles of care, gossip, advice, and surveillance.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
06“MOH-go chips”

Mogo is cassava, often cut and fried as chips. In East African Asian communities — and in diasporic neighborhoods in London — mogo is a familiar snack that signals a specifically East African South Asian route rather than a generic “Indian food” stereotype. The line helps place the song inside an East African Asian British social world.

07“coconut jokes”

A pointed insider joke about being “brown on the outside, white on the inside.” The song uses it warmly but knowingly: this is the kind of teasing that can only really function inside a community that already understands the codes of assimilation, class performance, and cultural authenticity.

08“In Britain ‘Asian’”

This is one of the song’s central conceptual hinges. In Britain, “Asian” commonly points toward South Asian communities; in the U.S., the word is often heard differently, which can make South Asian identity feel displaced or made secondary. The line captures the weird relief of being understood without a glossary.

09“chai twice”

A joke about café culture turning “chai” into the redundant phrase “chai tea” or the diluted coffee-shop object “chai tea latte.” Since chai already means tea, the phrase doubles the noun while often stripping away the texture of actual household spice, boiling, and patience. The song treats that commercial version as both funny and faintly absurd.

10“cardamom”

The reference grounds the song’s tea talk in actual kitchen practice. This is not branding-language chai but the real spice logic of home preparation: kettle on, milk in, cardamom in the pot. The line also marks a difference between domestic South Asian tea knowledge and flattened café menus.

11“Goodness Gracious Me”

The BBC sketch show is a key British Asian cultural touchstone. Referencing it signals a whole generation of jokes, accents, and social observations that British Asian audiences often recognize instantly. In the song, it stands for the pleasure of not having to over-explain a reference because the room already gets the channel.

12“EastEnders” / “Ilford”

This pairing jokes about the mismatch between televised British life and actually existing multicultural London. Saying EastEnders had more white people than the speaker had ever seen in Ilford turns representation into a neighborhood-level reality check. Ilford here becomes shorthand for the ordinary density of British Asian life that mainstream TV kept underrepresenting.

13“Rishi, Priti”

A deliberately compact name-drop. It points to Rishi Sunak and Priti Patel, two prominent British politicians from families with East African Asian routes, using their names as a kind of shorthand for how recognizable that migration history is in Britain — even if the speaker’s own version of it is politically and emotionally very different.

14“sari” / “Bet-ta, stand still”

These phrases place the listener in the choreography of a family wedding: borrowed clothing, slipping pleats, elders correcting technique, and affection arriving as command. “Beta” is a Gujarati and broader South Asian term of endearment meaning something like “child” or “dear one,” but here it comes with comic authority.

15“Lengha”

The long skirt-and-blouse outfit commonly worn for weddings and festive occasions across South Asia. The line turns clothing into social semiotics: each fabric, color, and adjustment carries judgment, admiration, or gossip.

16“Not too much sugar”

This is a tiny but telling cultural correction. The joke is not that chai must be ascetic; it is that proper chai has balance. The line quietly distinguishes household taste from over-sweetened café versions and from the idea that “authenticity” is just maximal spice and sugar.

17“samosas”

Here samosas function less as generic “Indian food” than as part of the wedding-hall snack table: familiar, communal, and ordinary enough not to need explanation. The track repeatedly insists that everyday cultural objects feel most powerful when they are allowed to stay ordinary.

18“Not foreign” / “another frequency”

The outro condenses the whole song’s thesis. The speaker is not announcing exotic difference; she is insisting on a different register of familiarity. “Another frequency” suggests radio tuning: same city, same air, different channel. It’s a softer answer than the album’s more combative songs, but no less exact.
