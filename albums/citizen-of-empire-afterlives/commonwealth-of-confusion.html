Commonwealth of Confusion

“Commonwealth of Confusion” is the big zoom-out song on Citizen of Empire.

Listen / buy this song on Bandcamp ↗
About this song

“Commonwealth of Confusion” is the big zoom-out song on Citizen of Empire. Where earlier tracks answer a classroom question, a border form, or an American stereotype one at a time, this track widens the frame until the whole post-imperial stage becomes visible at once. The title itself is the thesis: the British Empire may be formally over, but the Commonwealth-shaped afterlife of naming, paperwork, language, migration, border policy, nostalgia, and exclusion is still generating confusion for the people forced to live inside it.

The chorus turns that confusion into a system, not a personality flaw. “Same crown, different exclusions” argues that old imperial hierarchy survives by changing costume. English remains the shared language; the categories, passports, surnames, and gatekeeping logic remain too; only the official explanation changes. The song’s hook — “Now the whole world’s moving and they call it invasion” — is the central reversal. Migration is treated by politicians and media as a shocking event, even when the routes, crises, and displacements were built by older structures of empire, war, extraction, and global inequality.

The verses move like a camera. Verse 1 links the schoolyard to airport security and then to the family archive, especially the idea of a clerk-made surname or colonial administrative label coming back later as a reason for suspicion. Verse 2 shifts into spectacle and satire: Brexit tea-cup panic, Bridgerton fantasy, royal brand management, Black Sabbath’s “War Pigs,” Taiwan tension, Putin’s revanchist border logic, and North Korean nuclear brinkmanship all appear as pieces of one world system where elites posture while ordinary people absorb the cost.

Verse 3 makes the joke unbearable by widening it beyond identity and into survival. Syria, Ukraine, South Sudan, Sudan, Britain’s visa restrictions, Yemen’s proxy politics, and climate-driven precarity all collapse into one argument: the category “migrant” is never as stable as nationalist rhetoric pretends. The song insists that war, heat, flood, and state failure can convert almost anyone into the kind of displaced person respectable publics prefer to imagine as permanently elsewhere.

Musically, the track is conceived as an anthem rather than a battle-rap skit: a chant-like chorus, crisp drums, cinematic scale, and emotionally charged but controlled delivery. In the album arc it functions as the final synthesis before “Reply All.” The earlier songs each attack one bad question or one bad category; this song shows the entire apparatus that produced those questions in the first place.

Artist’s annotations

Choose an underlined lyric, or open an annotation below.

01“Commonwealth of confusion”

Literal: the title names a shared condition of post-imperial disorder. Context: the Commonwealth is the loose association of many former British imperial territories, so the phrase immediately invokes the empire’s administrative afterlife. Wordplay: it twists the polite, official sound of “Commonwealth” into something unstable and accusatory. Why it matters: the song is not about one misunderstanding; it is about a whole geopolitical system that keeps producing them. Canon link: this is the panoramic counterpart to the more focused questions asked in “3 Migrations Deep,” “A Tribe Called 44,” and “What Tribe?”

02“Same crown, different exclusions”

Literal: the monarchy’s symbolic center remains, but the mechanisms of exclusion keep changing form. Context: the line frames modern border policy, migration rhetoric, and racial sorting as successors to imperial hierarchy rather than clean breaks from it. Wordplay: “crown” works both as the literal British Crown and as shorthand for sovereign authority. Why it matters: it compresses the song’s main argument into six words. Canon link: Citizen of Empire repeatedly treats empire as inherited infrastructure, not finished history.

03“They kept the language”

Literal: English remains the governing shared medium. Context: the former empire’s language still organizes law, bureaucracy, media, schooling, and migration paperwork across many of its afterlives. Wordplay: “kept” sounds casual, but the lyric implies that language survived with far more than just vocabulary attached to it. Why it matters: the song argues that linguistic continuity helped carry institutional continuity too. Canon link: this echoes the album-wide concern with translation errors, administrative language, and how naming systems outlive formal rule.

04“Changed our names”

Literal: colonial or bureaucratic naming changed how families appeared on paper. Context: in the shared drafting history, this specifically reaches toward the narrator’s family name being a British administrative inheritance rather than an original, stable surname. Wordplay: the line links renaming to both violence and paperwork. Why it matters: it explains why later suspicion is not accidental; the state first writes the name, then interrogates the person carrying it. Canon link: this connects directly to “Protectorate Flow,” “Reply All,” and the album’s recurring concern with forms, surnames, and declarations.

05“searched us for the proof of it”

Literal: people are made to prove the very foreignness the system helped produce. Context: this fits both airport/security logic and the broader social demand that mixed or diasporic people explain themselves on command. Wordplay: “proof” means evidence, but also implies a courtroom burden or an admin test. Why it matters: the song keeps showing how institutions manufacture ambiguity and then punish people for inhabiting it. Canon link: A-Thrice often writes bureaucracy as a machine that creates the problem it later claims to solve.

06“Now the whole world’s moving and they call it invasion”

Literal: mass movement gets rhetorically recoded as threat. Context: the line answers anti-immigrant politics in Britain, the U.S., Canada, Australia, and elsewhere, especially when conflict, austerity, or housing crisis are displaced onto migrants. Wordplay: the punch comes from the contrast between a simple verb (“moving”) and a militarized noun (“invasion”). Why it matters: this is the chorus’s moral pivot — movement is ordinary, but the language used to describe it is loaded and strategic. Canon link: it expands the stereotype critique of “H-1B-Sides” into a global argument.

Further artist notes
  • Target of satire: the song is not mocking migrants, refugees, or displaced people. It is mocking imperial amnesia, nationalist nostalgia, royal image management, media panic language, and the bourgeois fantasy that global movement is surprising when the modern world was built by forced and managed movement.
  • Structural design: the repeated chorus behaves like a thesis being tested under new evidence. Each verse enlarges what the chorus means — first identity and paperwork, then pageantry and geopolitics, then war, climate, and universal precarity.
  • Camera logic: the song is written like a series of rapid cuts rather than one stable scene. Schoolyard, airport, palace, television, briefing room, border queue, and evacuation line all become part of one shared architecture.
  • Why the place names matter: Akobo, Kordofan, Taiwan, and Yemen stop the track from sounding like generic doom-scroll commentary. A-Thrice keeps naming actual places so that the song stays accountable to history rather than floating above it.
  • Cross-song links: Verse 1 folds back into “3 Migrations Deep” and “A Tribe Called 44.” The surname and paperwork logic point toward “Protectorate Flow.” The widened moral argument about categories, crisis, and future prepares the controlled final answer of “Reply All.”
  • Interpretive guardrail: if the song sounds sweeping, that is intentional. The track is not saying every conflict is identical. It is saying that old imperial structures, renamed institutions, and modern nationalist politics keep producing linked forms of confusion about who belongs, who moves, and who gets treated as a threat.
