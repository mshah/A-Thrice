About this song

“What Tribe?” is the moment in Citizen of Empire where A-Thrice stops politely correcting the record and starts putting the question itself on trial. The song takes the lazy prompt — “What tribe are you?” — and treats it not as innocent curiosity but as a miniature border checkpoint: a demand to simplify a diasporic life into something primitive, legible, and entertaining. Where “A Tribe Called 44” answers ignorance with a historical comeback, “What Tribe?” tears apart the grammar of the question and exposes the racial illiteracy, geographic confusion, and bureaucratic violence hidden inside it.

The structure matters as much as the bars. The song opens like a bulletin — dates, raids, arrest numbers, a Supreme Court hearing on birthright citizenship — and then mutates into interrogation theater. A sarcastic refrain gives way to a mock interview, then a cross-examination, then a darker beat-switch section where the performance drops its smile and names the larger machinery: ICE raids, executive orders, whiteness as the unmarked default, and the spectacle of a nation celebrating 250 years of freedom while still revising its guest list. The song’s joke is that the questioner thinks they are making small talk. The song hears customs, paperwork, and eligibility.

Musically and rhetorically, “What Tribe?” is built as battle-rap satire with a cold administrative spine. The hook keeps taunting the listener — “Depends who’s asking” — because the song insists that identity is never being asked about neutrally. Every “where are you really from?” carries a map, a hierarchy, and a fantasy of who gets to be called “just American.” The lyric’s wordplay stays nimble and funny, but the target is precise: not immigrants, not migrants, not mixed or diasporic people, but the people and institutions that keep acting shocked by identities history already made possible.

In the album arc, the original Citizen of Empire concept places “What Tribe?” just before the record turns decisively from personal misrecognition to structural critique. That sequencing makes it the confrontation peak of the opening act. It still belongs to the world of rude questions and social absurdity, but it is already leaning toward “Protectorate Flow,” where the routes, documents, and imperial systems behind those questions are laid out explicitly. “What Tribe?” is therefore both a takedown and a hinge: the last great argument about being misread before the album starts naming the architecture that taught people how to misread in the first place.

01“January 27, 2026”
  • Literal meaning: The song starts with a date-stamp, like a news brief or legal filing.
  • Reference / context: The opening date points to Reuters coverage of the Trump administration’s escalated immigration enforcement operations in major U.S. cities. The date makes the song explicitly contemporary rather than a vague social commentary.
  • Wordplay / double meaning: A song about being casually questioned opens with bureaucratic precision. The date reads like an incident report, telling the listener that what follows is not random small talk but part of an official climate.
  • Why it matters: The intro refuses nostalgia. It places the listener inside a live enforcement atmosphere before the first sarcastic line lands.
  • Connection to album / canon: Citizen of Empire repeatedly insists that identity questions are shaped by institutions, not just by bad manners. The date stamp is one more way of turning biography into paperwork.

02“746 a day”
  • Literal meaning: ICE arrest rates are summarized numerically.
  • Reference / context: This points to Reuters reporting on the pace of ICE arrests under the Trump administration, where the number functions as a compressed statistical image of enforcement escalation.
  • Wordplay / double meaning: The stripped-down number feels colder than a descriptive sentence. It sounds like quota logic — people rendered into throughput.
  • Why it matters: The song’s argument is that dehumanization often begins with counting. By the time the interview starts, the listener has already heard one way bodies get converted into administrative units.
  • Connection to album / canon: Numbers, categories, and systems recur across the album as instruments that claim neutrality while managing exclusion.

03“birthright citizenship”
  • Literal meaning: The intro names the legal doctrine that grants citizenship at birth to almost everyone born in the United States.
  • Reference / context: The line points to the Trump administration’s effort to restrict birthright citizenship and the Supreme Court arguments over whether such an order could survive the Fourteenth Amendment and the precedent of United States v. Wong Kim Ark.
  • Wordplay / double meaning: “Birthright” sounds ancient, almost royal, but here it becomes a contested administrative status. The song highlights the absurdity of treating birth itself like a revocable permit.
  • Why it matters: This line widens the song from rude speech to constitutional stakes. The question “What tribe?” is suddenly not only social ignorance; it is part of a broader battle over who counts as belonging by law.
  • Connection to album / canon: Citizen of Empire constantly contrasts inherited human life with inherited imperial paperwork. Birthright citizenship is exactly that collision.

04“America turns 250”
  • Literal meaning: The song notes the United States’ 250th anniversary year.
  • Reference / context: The lyric taps the symbolism of the 2026 semiquincentennial — a huge national self-celebration — and then undercuts it by asking who still gets treated as suspect inside that celebration.

05“Depends who’s asking”

  • Reference / context: The refrain insists there is no neutral version of the prompt. Some listeners ask out of curiosity, some out of surveillance, some out of entitlement, and some out of hostility.

  • Connection to album / canon: The same move appears all over Citizen of Empire: the system is forced to explain itself instead of merely demanding explanation from the person it misreads.

Further artist notes

Narrator / artist voice: The song uses a heightened performance voice — sarcastic, theatrical, battle-rap driven — but its political stakes are sincere. The satire targets anti-immigrant small talk, bureaucratic whiteness, nationalist gatekeeping, and the fantasy that American identity is neutral until a brown person says it out loud.

Album function: The original Citizen of Empire concept positions “What Tribe?” as the last major confrontation before the album darkens into structural history. That is why the song feels louder, pettier, and funnier than “Protectorate Flow,” even though the two tracks are tightly connected. One attacks the social question; the next attacks the historical machinery that produced it.

Motif note: The song is packed with A-Thrice’s recurring system words — map, customs, approved, legal, receipts, quotas, memo, paperwork, property. Even at its most playful, the lyric keeps translating social insult into institutional vocabulary.

Scale note: One of the track’s sharpest tricks is scale-shifting. It moves from one rude question, to a cocktail-party checkpoint, to ICE numbers, to constitutional doctrine, to a national 250th-anniversary spectacle. That widening frame is what turns the song from clapback into diagnosis.
