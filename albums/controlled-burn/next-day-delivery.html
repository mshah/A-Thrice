About this song

“Next Day Delivery” is the album’s logistics song: a fast, pleasurable tour of the machinery required to make a single impulsive tap feel frictionless. The narrator begins as a consumer triggered by scarcity messaging and free-delivery thresholds, then reveals an unusually intimate knowledge of route optimization, delivery windows, vehicle loads and reverse logistics. The machinery is not presented as stupid. She genuinely admires it.

That admiration is the song’s sharpest political choice. Route optimization can reduce miles, fuel, fleet needs and delivery cost; the critique is therefore not “algorithms are bad.” It is the narrower and more difficult question embedded in the pre-chorus: “Optimized the journey / Never asked what for.” A system can become extraordinarily good at satisfying an objective without asking whether the objective itself deserves to scale.

The second half reverses the chain. A dress arrives with remarkable precision, does not fit, receives a refund and enters the messier world of returns. Returned clothing can be restocked, marked down, sold through outlets, liquidated, donated, recycled or otherwise disposed of; the economics of inspection and handling affect which path wins. “Reverse logistics / Very nice” keeps the narrator inside the system: she can notice the absurdity and still appreciate the workflow.

The track strongly extends Citizen of Empire: Empire Infrastructure’s language of routes, supply chains and software. There, logistics reveals historical architecture. Here the same systems literacy enters consumer convenience. The question changes from “who built these routes?” to “what do we ask these routes to do now?”

Artist’s annotations

Choose an underlined lyric, or open an annotation below.

01“Little red badge says / Only two in stock”

Interface motif

The red badge turns scarcity into interface urgency. Like warning colors elsewhere on the album, red asks for immediate behavior—except the desired behavior here is purchase. Safety alerts and conversion design occupy the same visual nervous system.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
02“Free delivery / Spend another ten”

Wordplay + system cost

“Free” is conditional on additional consumption. The threshold nudges the narrator to add an item she calls useless. The bridge later reopens the word “free” to ask which costs—handling, labor, fuel, packaging and warehouse time—were moved off the checkout screen.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
03“Warehouse wakes at three / Somewhere in the dark / A system starts for me”

One private click triggers distributed industrial choreography. “For me” makes the scale comic and seductive: labor, warehousing, routing and transport align around one consumer’s small desire. This is invisible infrastructure becoming intimate.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
04“Pick it / Pack it / Scan it / Stack it / Label / Sort it / Route it / Track it”

Strong canon echo

The pre-chorus behaves like a workflow executing. Human labor and software states blur into the rhythm. Rather than describing a system from outside, the lyric starts to sound like the system, an A-Thrice technique also used for bureaucratic pipelines in earlier work.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
05“Fulfilment centre / Still not fulfilling me”

Wordplay

A fulfilment centre processes orders; “fulfilment” also means emotional satisfaction. The pun separates logistical success from psychological endpoint. The centre can fulfil an order perfectly while leaving the desire that generated it unresolved.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
06“Doorstep dopamine”

Factual guardrail + metaphor

The phrase is cultural shorthand for the anticipation/reward loop of ordering, tracking, arrival and opening, not a clinical claim about neurotransmitter measurement. The countdown post-chorus performs the compulsion through “eight stops… seven… six… refresh.”

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
07“I used to optimize the routes / Every mile / Every stop”

Documented writing intent

The narrator suddenly speaks from inside the machinery rather than as an abstract critic. Delivery windows, vehicle loads, path choice and distance are recognizably transportation-management language. The verse deliberately takes an insider systems perspective.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
08“All that beautiful optimization / For a dress / I might not keep”

Factual context + thesis

“Beautiful” is sincere. Route optimization can reduce unnecessary miles, fuel and emissions, so the song refuses to make efficiency itself the villain. The absurdity lies in the objective chain: technical elegance is mobilized for a product whose use value may last only through a try-on.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
09“Optimized the journey / Never asked / What for”

Strong canon echo

This is the song’s thesis and a strong continuation of Citizen of Empire: Empire Infrastructure. That project rejects the idea that infrastructure is neutral and asks what objectives inherited routes serve. Here optimization answers “how efficiently?” but not “toward what end?” The moral question lives outside the objective function.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
10“Refund lands / Before it does”

Financial closure arrives before material closure. The consumer’s account is resolved while the object remains in a physical reverse-logistics process. The interface therefore gives the narrator permission to stop thinking before the garment’s story is finished.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
11“Maybe restock / Maybe markdown / Maybe outlet / Maybe not”

Factual guardrail

The lyric intentionally avoids the false claim that returned clothing is automatically destroyed. Returns can follow multiple resale and disposition paths. The uncertainty is the point: once the refund arrives, the consumer usually cannot see which branch wins.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
12“Reverse logistics / Very nice”

Performance + technical meaning

“Reverse logistics” names the systems that move products backward through a supply chain after returns. The line should sound genuinely impressed. The narrator remains implicated because she likes the machinery even while questioning what it enables.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
13“Too much handling / Too much cost / Perfectly good / Can still get lost”

Factual context

Returns create inspection, transport, labor and inventory costs. “Lost” is intentionally broader than physically misplaced: an item can become economically inconvenient enough to be diverted from straightforward resale.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
14“Free returns / Free delivery / Funny what ‘Free’ doesn’t mean”

Wordplay

The customer may pay no incremental shipping fee, but the activity still consumes resources. “Free” is a price displayed to one participant, not a description of a costless physical process.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
15“Do I need it? / … / What colour?”

Internal callback + complicity

The outro completes the consumer loop without moral conversion. The narrator begins with “No,” sees the entire system, and ends by asking for another variant. Like Polyester Girl’s “Does it come in black?”, knowledge and appetite remain in the same person.

Reading and references
  • U.S. EPA SmartWay — transportation management systems, routing and logistics optimization
  • European Environment Agency — returned / unsold textile pathways and processing costs
  • European Environment Agency — textiles and circular economy
  • Citizen of Empire: Empire Infrastructure — routes, supply chains and software (canon context)
Further artist notes
  • Voice distinction: consumer, systems insider and amused narrator coexist. None completely cancels the others.
  • Satire target: frictionless-consumption incentives and unexamined objectives, not route optimization or logistics workers.
  • Hidden movement: forward logistics in verses 1–2 becomes reverse logistics in verse 3, but the emotional user journey closes before the material journey does.
  • Canon bridge: Citizen of Empire treated routes and supply chains as historical infrastructure; this song inherits that literacy and asks a contemporary objective-function question.
  • Internal loop: “Do I need it? No” → “What colour?” pairs with Polyester Girl’s “Does it come in black?” as desire surviving knowledge.
