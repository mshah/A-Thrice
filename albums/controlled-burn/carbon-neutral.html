About this song

“Carbon Neutral” is Controlled Burn at its most openly satirical: a corporate sustainability campaign having a cheerful nervous breakdown. The narrator discovers that carbon guilt can be processed like a checkout option—tick a box, add a tree, move a decimal, receive moral confirmation—and immediately enjoys the convenience.

The second verse compresses several real histories of environmental branding. BP’s “Beyond Petroleum” era and Helios imagery are set against the Macondo disaster; Shell’s refinery-flower advertising becomes “flowers growing from the stack”; KLM’s “Fly Responsibly” language becomes part of an offset joke; Repsol’s leaf-and-pump imagery appears in the miniature ad-jingle. The point is not that every environmental program is fake. It is that branding can compress complicated transition claims into a visual feeling of cleanliness.

The bridge switches from advertising to carbon-accounting vocabulary: Scope 1, Scope 2 and Scope 3. Those categories are real tools for distinguishing direct, purchased-energy and value-chain emissions. “Scope one / Scope two / Scope three / Not me” turns useful accounting into a moral-dodging rhyme, exposing the gap between measuring responsibility and emotionally relocating it.

The break contains the album’s clearest direct callback to Panic by Design: “*Terms may apply.” In the earlier song, fine print and interface design turn consent into a managed user journey. Here the same phrase becomes a disclaimer beneath “carbon-free,” linking greenwashing to A-Thrice’s larger suspicion of reassuring labels whose conditions live somewhere smaller or out of sight.

Artist’s annotations

Choose an underlined lyric, or open an annotation below.

01“Would you like to offset that? / … / Thank you / You’re a good person”

Satire target

The checkout voice converts a complex climate decision into immediate moral validation. The satire is aimed at the emotional design of the transaction, not at the claim that every offset project is worthless.

02“Three quid extra, plant a tree / Now the sky forgives me”

Religious / accounting wordplay

Payment, absolution and forgiveness turn offsetting into a miniature indulgence. The joke is the temptation to treat a quantified transaction as moral erasure of the original activity.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
03“Lower carbon to my door / Felt so good / I ordered more”

Factual guardrail + satire

A rebound joke: a lower-impact or guilt-reducing option can make additional consumption feel easier. The lyric does not claim every lower-carbon delivery raises total emissions; it dramatizes the possibility that per-unit improvement and higher demand can coexist.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
04“Count it once / Count it twice / Move it somewhere / Out of sight”

Internal callback

Accounting joins Blue Bin’s disposal language. “Out of sight” can mean shifting emissions across scopes, suppliers, places or time horizons. The danger is not measurement; it is mistaking a relocated number for a resolved system.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
05“Add a tree / Subtract a flight / Move the decimal / Sleep at night”

Factual context + satire

The nursery-rhyme arithmetic deliberately oversimplifies carbon equivalence. Forestry projects and flight emissions raise timing, permanence and additionality questions that cannot be reduced to four easy operations.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
06“Green sun shining at the pump / ‘Beyond Petroleum’ up front”

Historical reference

BP adopted the green/yellow Helios identity around the era it promoted “beyond petroleum.” “Green sun” compresses that solar-looking visual language into one image, then the lyric places it beside Macondo to contrast brand aspiration and operating history.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
07“Macondo underneath the sea / Four million barrels disagree”

Historical reference

The Macondo well was the source of the 2010 Deepwater Horizon spill. U.S. EPA materials describe roughly four million barrels of oil flowing over 87 days. “Disagree” turns the physical spill into a rebuttal to the cleaner brand story above it.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
08“Flowers growing from the stack / Look, the oil is giving back”

Advertising reference

This references Shell advertising that depicted refinery chimneys emitting flowers, associated with an advertising-standard complaint about misleading environmental claims. The lyric makes the image literal enough to become absurd.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
09“Fly responsibly tonight / Add a forest to the flight”

Advertising / legal context

The phrase echoes airline sustainability advertising such as KLM’s “Fly Responsibly” campaign, parts of which were later found misleading by a Dutch court. The target is not the proposition that aviation can improve, but the fantasy of fully reconciled high-carbon activity through wording.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
10“Repsol / Leaf on the pump / Net zero / Fill it up”

Advertising ruling

A UK ASA ruling found a Repsol environmental ad misleading where green/renewable-fuel messaging lacked sufficient context about the company’s wider fossil-fuel activity and transition timeframe. “Fill it up” is the punchline: consumer behavior remains the same while the symbolic wrapper changes.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
11“Scope one / Scope two / Scope three / Not me”

Technical wordplay

GHG Protocol scopes distinguish direct emissions, purchased-energy emissions and other value-chain emissions. The categories are useful accounting tools; “Not me” turns them into a joke about moral outsourcing.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
12“Oil goes in / Promise comes out”

Strong canon echo

This strongly echoes Panic by Design’s input/output syntax, especially “Threat goes in, profit comes out” in Fear Economy. Here the machine transforms fossil input into branding output.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
13“Carbon-free* / *Terms may apply”

Direct canon callback

A deliberate direct callback to Panic by Design’s “Terms May Apply.” The asterisk does the same work: a clean headline lives above conditions the consumer may never inspect.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
14“I paid the extra three quid / So we’re even / Right?”

Outro meaning

The question mark prevents the song from ending in cynicism or absolution. The narrator wants the simple transaction to restore balance; that desire is part of why green reassurance can be so emotionally effective.

Further artist notes
  • Voice distinction: the narrator enjoys the moral convenience and knows it is too neat; the checkout voice acts like a character offering absolution.
  • Satire target: greenwashing and moral simplification—not every offset, biofuel, emissions inventory or corporate climate target.
  • “*Terms may apply” is a deliberate cross-catalog callback.
  • “Oil goes in / Promise comes out” structurally echoes Panic by Design’s systems-as-conversion-machine language.
  • The green leaf motif first appears domestically in Blue Bin, scales up into corporate identity here, and returns to packaging in Biodegradable.
