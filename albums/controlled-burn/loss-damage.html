Loss & Damage

“Loss & Damage” is the political center of the record.

Listen / buy this song on Bandcamp ↗
About this song

“Loss & Damage” is the political center of the record. The title comes from international climate policy, where “loss and damage” refers to harms associated with climate change that cannot simply be prevented or fully managed through mitigation and adaptation. The song translates that policy phrase into rooms, tickets, insurance, air conditioning, flood maps and the price of leaving.

The opening joke—calling a voluntary northbound escape from London heat “refugee” flight—is immediately corrected by the lyric itself. “I booked ahead,” “cooler postcode,” “softer bed” and “somewhere else to go” expose the narrator’s mobility as privilege rather than forced displacement. Her bad joke creates the real question: what does heat mean when one person can buy an exit and another has to stay inside it?

The chorus keeps the hazard constant and changes capacity: “Same storm / different price,” “same warning / different ticket,” “same planet / different options.” Climate-vulnerability research repeatedly shows that exposure, housing, insurance, infrastructure, savings and mobility are unevenly distributed, so a shared physical event does not create a shared human consequence.

The song’s bureaucratic language is intentionally cold. “Under review,” “claim denied,” “policy changed,” “flood map rearranged” make vulnerability sound like a file moving through a system. That continues both Citizen of Empire’s interest in mobility/paperwork and Panic by Design’s interest in calm institutional language at odds with human stakes.

Artist’s annotations

Choose an underlined lyric, or open an annotation below.

01“Loss and damage”

Policy reference

In UN climate negotiations, “loss and damage” refers to climate-related harms that go beyond what mitigation and adaptation can fully prevent or address, including extreme events and slow-onset impacts. The song puts people back inside an administrative phrase: roof, ticket, hotel, doorway and claim.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
02“Told my friends I’m now a refugee / They said, ‘See you when it rains’”

Narrator self-correction

The narrator casually borrows the language of forced displacement for an elective escape. The lyric immediately undermines the equivalence: her friends expect her return, and later lines reveal planning, money and somewhere else to go.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
03“I miss ten-degree summers / Grey sky, sideways rain”

Tone / irony

The usual complaint about northern weather is reversed. Cold wet summer becomes desirable because it represents relief. The song stays in the language of travel preference rather than turning the narrator into a policy lecturer.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
04“I call it fleeing / I booked ahead”

Privilege layer

Two lines perform the ethical correction. “Fleeing” borrows displacement intensity; “booked ahead” reveals planned mobility and infrastructure. The narrator can leave because transport, destination and budget exist for her.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
05“Same warning / Different ticket / Same heat / Different exit”

Strong canon echo

The hazard is held constant while access to exit changes. “Ticket” is literal rail fare and broader access: money, documents, insurance, social networks, spare rooms or second homes. It echoes Citizen of Empire’s mobility questions while changing the sorting mechanism.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
06“Some of us / Can leave the room / Some of us / Just live in it”

Metaphor

“Room” can be literal overheated housing, city, country or planet. The lyric reduces climate inequality to the simplest adaptation resource: exit. Mobility is something people possess unequally.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
07“Same storm / Different price”

Climate-vulnerability context

The physical hazard is not the whole disaster. Housing quality, savings, public infrastructure, insurance and recovery capacity shape the consequence. “Price” can be financial, bodily, cultural or permanent displacement.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
08“Somebody calls it weather / Somebody calls it life”

For someone with mobility and insurance, an extreme event can remain an episode. For someone repeatedly exposed, it becomes the structure of daily life. The line resists treating climate harm only as exceptional news.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
09“One house gets another roof / One disappears in rain”

Policy concept

The rhyme places recoverable damage beside irreversible loss. Insurance and capital can convert some damage into repair; other losses cannot be restored simply by replacing an object.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
10“Hotel lobby / Filtered air / Cold enough / To need a sleeve / Down the road / The power’s out”

Inequality image

Two microclimates coexist nearby. Wealth can purchase cooling and filtration while infrastructure failure leaves others exposed. The overcooled hotel makes unequal adaptation physically absurd.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
11“One flat’s got the air-con on / One’s got foil against the glass”

Both residents are adapting rationally, but with different resources. The satire is not aimed at the improvised foil solution; it is aimed at a system where private improvisation substitutes for equal protection.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
12“Insurance says / ‘Under review’ / Landlord says / ‘Nothing we can do’”

Strong canon echo

Procedural language performs emotional distance. “Under review” may be ordinary process language, but its calm grammar is wildly out of scale with someone waiting inside damage. This is strongly continuous with Panic by Design.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
13“Funny how ‘resilience’ sounds / When the choice is move instead”

Policy wordplay

Resilience is a legitimate planning concept, but the lyric asks who is being asked to be resilient and what options they actually have. Positive policy language can hide a transfer of burden onto individuals.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
14“Adaptation / Sounds so clean / Till you ask / Who gets to leave”

Album motif + policy context

Adaptation includes real protective measures, but none is distributed equally. “Clean” is also an album word associated with labels, surfaces and reassurance; here clean terminology conceals messy distributional politics.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
15“One family starts over / One can’t afford the train”

Structural resolution

The final chorus turns the narrator’s original rail journey into a privilege test. The train is no longer a quirky escape; it becomes the smallest imaginable unit of mobility that another family still cannot buy.

Reading and references
  • UNFCCC — loss and damage framework / Fund for Responding to Loss and Damage
  • IPCC AR6 Working Group II — vulnerability, adaptation gaps and unequal impacts
  • Citizen of Empire: Empire Infrastructure — mobility and paperwork (canon context)
  • Panic by Design — procedural institutional language (canon context)
Further artist notes
  • Voice distinction: the self-mocking “refugee” joke belongs to narrator voice; the artist voice appears in the immediate correction and unequal-exit structure.
  • Satire target: privileged self-dramatization and policy euphemism, not displaced people and not adaptation itself.
  • Policy guardrail: “loss and damage” is a specific climate-policy term, not a synonym for all climate action.
  • Canon bridge: Citizen of Empire asks who gets mobility and legibility; this song shifts the sorting mechanism from passports/categories to money, housing, insurance and infrastructure.
  • Internal architecture: “can leave the room” answers 39°’s “hotter inside”; Weather Warning later shows that everyone can receive the same alert even when exits remain unequal.
