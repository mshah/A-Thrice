Lockdown Vocabulary

“Lockdown Vocabulary” is one of the most intimate and quietly devastating songs on Panic by Design.

Listen / buy this song on Bandcamp ↗
About this song

“Lockdown Vocabulary” is one of the most intimate and quietly devastating songs on Panic by Design. If Visitor Badge looks at the visible theater of school security, Lockdown Vocabulary turns inward and listens to what that theater sounds like once it gets absorbed by children. The song is not mainly about the event of a lockdown. It is about the language around it: the euphemisms, commands, rehearsed phrases, and procedural words that children learn before they can fully understand why they are learning them.

That is what makes the title so strong. “Vocabulary” usually belongs to literacy, school growth, language acquisition, and childhood learning. But here the words being learned are emergency commands, perimeter language, shelter language, and codes of fear. The song’s ache comes from the mismatch between the innocence of language-learning and the violence of what the language is preparing children to survive.

This is one of the clearest examples of Panic by Design’s human cost. The album concept frames the record as a map of how fear becomes environment and governance. Lockdown Vocabulary shows the point where that environment reaches the child: panic becomes syntax, and safety becomes a script spoken before it is understood.

Artist’s annotations

Choose an underlined lyric, or open an annotation below.

01“Lockdown vocabulary”

The title is already a thesis. “Vocabulary” usually implies the healthy expansion of language — literacy, school, learning, development. Pairing it with “lockdown” is what gives the phrase its force. The song is about the intrusion of emergency procedure into the sphere of childhood learning. Children are being taught not just words, but a worldview of concealment, scanning, compliance, and threat.

02“Secure perimeter”

This is pure institutional phrasing. It sounds technical, remote, and procedural, which is exactly why it is so haunting in a song about children. The phrase belongs to police, school administration, emergency response, and official bulletins. In the song it becomes part of a child’s lexicon. That is the injury: adult command language is entering childhood before childhood has the context to metabolize it.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
03“Shelter in place”

A major phrase in the song and one of the most loaded euphemisms in the whole album. On one level it is a practical directive. On another, it is a phrase that sounds soothing and domestic while containing fear, confinement, and danger. “Shelter” sounds protective; “in place” sounds orderly. Together they soften the violence of what the phrase is actually asking bodies to do.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
04“Return to class”

This phrase is devastating precisely because of its banality. Like “all clear,” it is institutional closure language. It implies continuity and routine: the educational machine can resume. But in the emotional logic of these songs, “return to class” is never a full answer to what the body has just been asked to hold.

05“Passed from adult mouth to youth”

The focus is not on abstract “students,” but on children physically learning to form these phrases. That makes institutional language intimate and tragic. These are words too heavy for the bodies learning to say them.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
06“How to hold a body still and call that being smart”

Emergency language becomes physical instruction. Children learn how to arrange, quiet and control their bodies; the song connects that bodily training to the album’s larger idea that fear is designed into routines and spaces.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
07“before they’re taught what safety means”

This is one of the song’s sharpest moral lines. It points to a terrible inversion: children learn emergency commands before they receive a stable concept of safety itself. The line isn’t just saying that childhood is now sadder or more fearful. It is saying that the educational sequence has been rearranged by violence and its institutional response.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
08“vocabulary” as school-language

The title word also matters because it belongs to assessment, literacy standards, lesson plans, developmental milestones, and school metrics. That creates a bitter irony: the school is still teaching language, but the semantic field is now perimeter, shelter, alert, drill, secure. The song is about emergency as curricular atmosphere.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
09“instruction”

This is one of the song’s recurring emotional tensions. Instruction is supposed to be neutral, educational, useful. In the lockdown context, instruction becomes survival choreography. The same soft school mechanisms that teach reading or lining up now teach what to do with your body when harm is imagined nearby.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
10“Friday: drills”

“Drill” is one of the most morally unstable words in this part of the album. It can mean practice, preparedness, repetition, routine. It can also imply penetration, force, militarization, and repetitive conditioning. In a school setting, a “drill” is supposed to reduce panic by familiarizing children with procedure. The song asks what happens when that familiarity itself becomes a wound.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
11“quiet”

Quiet in these songs is never just absence of sound. It is often enforced, tactical, or emotionally loaded. In Lockdown Vocabulary, quiet becomes part of the child’s training. Silence is no longer simply classroom discipline or concentration; it becomes a protective behavior. That shift is one of the song’s deepest sadnesses.

12“Little shoes on tile lines, backpack straps in little fists”

This image refuses abstraction. The song narrows big social issues down to scale. Safety protocol is one thing in policy language; it is another in relation to children being told how to fold themselves into corners, silence, or stillness.

13“Teacher says, “Stay calm now,” like calm can close a space”

A crucial emotional detail. The teacher is not a villain or militarized authority figure here. The teacher voice is often trying to remain calm, kind, and usable. That makes the song sharper, not softer: ordinary care is forced to carry unbearable material. The teacher becomes the channel through which institutional fear reaches children in softened form.

14“Intercom voice comes soft and flat and every child goes still”

This continues an A-Thrice pattern across the album: power and fear often speak softly. The school does not need to sound monstrous for the reality to be monstrous. The calm voice is part of the mechanism that makes emergency teachable.

15“Funny what gets taught to children before they’re taught what safety means”

One of the album’s recurring moves is to examine how language labels conditions. “Safety” is rarely rejected outright; instead the songs keep asking what gets named safety, who has to enact it, and what the naming conceals. In Lockdown Vocabulary, calling something “safety” does not erase the emotional fact that the child is learning fear first.

16“what the lesson never asked it to”

This is one of the most important lines in the song. It suggests that the child’s body or mind has learned something beyond the intended “lesson.” The official lesson is procedure. The unofficial lesson is vigilance, anticipatory fear, and a changed relation to school space. That distinction mirrors the whole album’s interest in what systems say they are teaching versus what they actually produce.

17“lesson”

A painful word in this context. Lessons are supposed to belong to ordinary educational growth. When emergency protocol becomes a “lesson,” the school system is trying to absorb fear into its existing pedagogical framework. The song shows how inadequate that assimilation is.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
18“before”

A small but powerful structural word in this song. Several of its emotional claims depend on sequence: before they know safety, before they understand law, before they can name the threat fully, before the institution can explain itself. The tragedy is developmental. The words arrive too early.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
19“shelter”

Like “care” or “security,” this is a morally attractive word. That is why it works so well in the song. “Shelter” sounds warm, domestic, kind, and necessary. The song does not deny that. It asks what happens when that language is attached to institutional fear-conditioning so early that children learn the shell of reassurance before the substance of safety.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
20“perimeter”

A militarized or law-enforcement term smuggled into childhood speech. Perimeters belong to tactical management, not ordinary classroom life. The moment a child knows the phrase, something has already changed in the world around them.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
21“mouth” / “body” split

One of the song’s most important underlying structures is the difference between what the child can say and what the body can carry. The mouth learns the phrase. The body learns the fear. That split makes the song richer than a simple protest statement; it is about embodiment, not just politics.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
22“Teacher pulls the window shade like she’s done this all before”

The line signals normalization through repetition. The horrifying thing is not novelty but institutional familiarity.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
23“Substitute still learning names”

A small but devastating detail. Emergency procedure arrives before relational knowledge. Institutional fear precedes ordinary human recognition.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
24“vocabulary” as inheritance

A deeper layer in the title is that vocabulary is how a culture passes its priorities on. The phrase implies that a generation is being handed a lexicon of emergency as part of ordinary civic inheritance. The song is asking what kind of society teaches perimeter-speech as childhood fluency.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
25“secure”

This word appears across the album in different guises: secure perimeter, feeling secure, security theater, secure language. In Lockdown Vocabulary, “secure” is one of those words children can learn phonetically long before they can test whether the world it names is real.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
26“place”

As in “shelter in place,” the word “place” matters because the album is obsessed with how environments shape feeling. Place is no longer neutral location; it becomes fixed position, assigned body-space, managed relation to threat. The song is quietly about the politics of where bodies are told to be.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
27“Secure perimeter” / “Shelter in place”

A subtler layer in the song is that institutional language often works like ritual speech: commands, repeated phrases, rehearsed responses. That resembles education, religion, drill, and bureaucracy all at once. The repeated phrases in the song point to that ritual dimension.

LINK TO THIS NOTE ↗
28“safety means”

The phrase “what safety means” is more philosophical than it first appears. The song is not only saying children are frightened. It is asking how meaning itself gets built under these conditions. What does “safety” come to mean if one learns it through commands, drills, silence, and hiding?

29“Words no child should have to use”

The song is about premature absorption. The child is not first given context and then language. The child is given language first and left to grow into the dread it implies.

Voice and album connections

Narrator voice:

The speaker here is close, restrained, and mournful rather than openly caustic. The song speaks with witness rather than spectacle. That tonal difference matters because it keeps the track from becoming a slogan.

Target of critique:

The song critiques the social and institutional reality in which schoolchildren learn emergency-command language as part of ordinary life. It is especially focused on how euphemism, procedure, and educational tone can make deeply abnormal conditions feel routine.

What the song is not saying:

  • It is not mocking teachers or school staff.
  • It is not denying that safety instructions may be practically necessary.
  • It is not reducing the issue to one melodramatic scene.
  • It is not treating children as symbols only; the song is deeply interested in lived scale.

What it is saying:

A society has changed profoundly when children learn lockdown phrases as part of ordinary schooling, and that change cannot be understood only in terms of policy. It has linguistic, developmental, emotional, and bodily consequences.

Connection to Visitor Badge:

Visitor Badge shows the visible theater of school safety. Lockdown Vocabulary shows the linguistic residue of that same world inside children’s speech and memory.

Connection to All Clear:

All Clear shows what remains in the body after official closure. Lockdown Vocabulary shows how the body starts being trained long before any closure phrase arrives.

Connection to Fear Economy:

Fear Economy explains the larger system that profits from permanent alertness. Lockdown Vocabulary shows how that alertness gets planted early, before a child can even define the terms they are repeating.

Together, these songs create a sequence:

Visitor Badge = visible ritual

Lockdown Vocabulary = internalized language

All Clear = bodily aftermath
