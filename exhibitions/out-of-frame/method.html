The method

Reconstructing
with limits.

The archival photograph is evidence. The surrounding world is an interpretation. We make the difference visible.

01 / Two kinds of image

What survives.
What we infer.

Each study begins with an actual historic photograph. A wider panorama is then developed through research into participants, places, material culture and the circumstances of its making.

These artworks are not recovered wider photographs. The surrounding imagery is a contemporary reconstruction. It can illustrate plausible people and actions without claiming that every detail existed at that precise instant.

In Buffalo Corner, the historical photograph of five men and three buffalo heads sits within a wider reconstruction of camp work, transport and landscape. In The Royal Ring, George V remains visible within the original royal hunting photograph, surrounded by an interpreted expedition scene. In The Host and the Trophy, the Pollen hunting-party photograph anchors a reconstruction of elephant care, transport and princely hospitality. None is a recovered wider exposure or a record of a single simultaneous moment.

02 / Reading the evidence

Four levels of certainty.

A

Visible source

Details observable in the selected archival photograph itself.

B

Same event

Evidence tied to the specific event, place or expedition, without necessarily proving this exact camera view.

C

Context

Contemporary practice, geographic information or comparative historical reference.

D

Interpretation

Artistic choices about location, moment, appearance or action that the evidence cannot establish.

The expedition may document a person’s role (B), while their precise position in the reconstructed scene remains an artistic decision (D).

03 / The viewer’s role

Encounter first.
Evidence second.

The initial encounter begins with the image, not an explanation. Visitors do not need prior knowledge of safari logistics, Nepalese hunting rings or the social structures of either expedition.

Interactive annotations then offer a second reading: who is credited, who worked, what animals endured, and how photography transformed an elite pursuit into public memory.

The series asks what changes when a photograph of a powerful individual is viewed alongside the larger human and nonhuman operation that sustained the event. A photograph also acquired meaning after the exposure—through captions, publishing, albums, recipients and later archives.

04 / Historical publication frames

Two editions.
One exposure.

Not every inquiry requires an artist’s reconstructed panorama. The Burges rhinoceros case note compares two genuinely different published crops of the same historical exposure in 1898 and 1900. Neither is presented as the complete original negative.
