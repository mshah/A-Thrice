Historical background · Study 01

The hunt and
the photograph.

Five men stand together because their roles converged in a hunt. The photograph preserves the trophy; the historical record reveals the expedition behind it.

Occasion

Location
Hugh Heatley’s Kamiti ranch, British East Africa (present-day Kenya)

Library of Congress photograph; African Game Trails (1910)

Why Roosevelt went to Africa

In March 1909, shortly after leaving the presidency of the United States, Theodore Roosevelt set out for East Africa with his son Kermit. Their expedition combined big-game hunting with the collection of animals for the Smithsonian Institution. Private donations supported three museum naturalists: Edgar A. Mearns, responsible principally for birds; John Alden Loring, for smaller mammals; and Edmund Heller, for large mammals and specimen preparation. The venture was at once a sporting journey, a scientific collecting enterprise and a carefully recorded public undertaking.

Why these five men stood together

In May, the party visited the ranch of Hugh H. Heatley near Kamiti. Heatley was the host; Richard John Cuninghame was the professional hunter and safari organiser; Theodore Roosevelt led the expedition; Kermit Roosevelt hunted and served as its official photographer; and Edmund Heller was a naturalist charged with the preparation of large-mammal specimens. They met in this frame because the hunt required different forms of authority, hospitality, expertise and work. At their feet lie three buffalo trophy heads, arranged for the camera before a tent.

The composition makes their names and the animals’ remains easy to remember. It tells us much less about the skinners, carriers, cooks and attendants whose work continued before and after the sitting. Roosevelt’s account describes the use of an ox-wagon to transport meat and skins, a working camp and, when the party broke camp, porters dancing before their departure. These are documented parts of the Kamiti visit, but they were not necessarily present at the same moment as the photograph.

Who arranged the photograph?

We know who posed. We do not know who directed the arrangement or pressed the shutter. The Library of Congress credits Kermit Roosevelt as the photographer, and the Smithsonian identifies him as the expedition’s official photographer; yet Kermit himself stands in the group. The catalogue credit therefore cannot, by itself, settle the operator of this particular exposure. Other photographs from the Kamiti expedition appeared in Roosevelt’s 1910 account, African Game Trails, but this exact five-man portrait has not yet been matched to those early illustrations. The Library of Congress also records a 1919 copyright registration for the selected print—not a date for the hunt.

Photographic history · The photograph's second life

From safari to public memory

February 1910Related Kamiti images published in Scribner's Magazine
14 May 1919Copyright registration for the selected print

Roosevelt's adventures reached magazine readers through photographs as well as prose. The February 1910 Kamiti article included separate images of porters dancing and Edmund Heller preparing game heads for shipment. Those people and tasks were visible in the printed record, even as Roosevelt remained the main narrator.

The selected five-man photograph has a different, documented print history: the Library of Congress records a 1919 copyright deposit by Charles Scribner's Sons. That date does not date the exposure, and the exact portrait has not yet been confirmed in the February 1910 issue. The catalogue credits Kermit Roosevelt, who is himself pictured; its actual shutter operator remains unknown.

Historical publication, exposure and modern archival description are separate layers of evidence. Companion expedition photographs are not proof that their subjects stood outside this particular frame.

What the wider field asks us to see

Heatley’s property was a working cattle-and-dairy ranch with cultivated land, close to the papyrus marshes described in Roosevelt’s narrative. Buffalo Corner: The Wider Camp places these documented settings beside the labour and social life of the expedition. Its colour scenes are not a newly discovered photograph or a precise map of the camp: they bring together different moments, comparisons and artistic interpretation to question why the trophy group became the image that survived.
