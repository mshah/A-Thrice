Historical background · Study 03

The host and
the portrait.

A photographed tiger became an occasion for guests, animals and rulers to be remembered. The people who organised the hunt have not all received the same recognition.

The maharaja's invitation

Maharaja Nripendra Narayan ruled Cooch Behar as a princely state under British paramountcy. The surviving hunting album associates him with visiting guests including the Prince and Princess of Pless. Organising such an occasion required access to hunting grounds, elephants, staff, camp facilities and a political household capable of sustaining a large travelling party.

That does not establish which person in this particular tiger photograph is the maharaja. The selected print has no verified sitter-by-sitter caption. His role as host is documented for the album and visits, not a warrant to attach his name to a face by resemblance.

The people already in the frame

The project's working inventory distinguishes thirteen foreground people (G01–G13), eight elevated figures (R01–R08), and five conservatively distinguishable elephants (E01–E05). These are provisional research labels, not identities or an official census. Overlap and the quality of the surviving reproduction can affect the count.

Several elevated figures occupy forward positions consistent with elephant handlers. Their presence challenges a simple story that workers were entirely outside the camera's view. Some participants are visible but unnamed: visibility is not the same as recognition.

A trophy without a securely named hunter

The tiger lies in the foreground before a posed group. The Bonhams catalogue mentions a separate tiger associated with Pollen, but does not establish that the pictured animal is the same. Nor can we determine its individual life, the location of its killing, or the person credited with the shot.

Peter Burges described conventions in which a tiger's first successful bullet could determine sporting credit, with the maharaja sometimes arbitrating disputes. That is evidence about a practice in his circle, not proof of a dispute over this animal.

The elephants and the institution behind them

The elephants' howdahs and forward-seated riders point towards skilled preparation and handling. Accounts of Cooch Behar hunting describe mahouts, mates, equipment fitting and the state pilkhana—the elephant establishment that maintained animals and staff beyond any one hunt.

Suniti Devi's memoir describes fifty-two elephants in 1900. That number concerns a different year and cannot be used as a count for this photograph or the 1895–96 expedition. Her memories of care for elephants also differ from Burges's descriptions of risk and coercive control; both are attributed perspectives, neither a complete account.

Photographic history · The photograph's second life

Who made the picture? Who kept it?

1895–96Hunting-party album associated with the maharaja and guests
1921Suniti Devi recalls photography and disputes some later attribution of camp images
9 April 2008Pollen's photographic album recorded in a Bonhams auction

Stephen Hungerford Pollen compiled the album. That is not evidence that he operated the camera for this photograph. In her memoir, Maharani Suniti Devi recalled Sir Benjamin Simpson photographing shooting camps and complained that another writer had published camp photographs as his own. Her account cannot identify the maker of this particular Pollen image or the author she criticised.

The album's later sale preserves a record of circulation, but the selected exposure's original caption, first audience and photographer remain open questions.
