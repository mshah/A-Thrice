Why the king went to Nepal

George V travelled to India in 1911 as the newly crowned British king-emperor. On 12 December, the Delhi Durbar presented him before India’s princes and rulers. Soon afterward he accepted an invitation to hunt in Nepal’s Tarai, a region of forest and grassland along the Indian border. His host was Chandra Shumsher Jung Bahadur Rana, Nepal’s powerful hereditary prime minister. A hunting visit planned during George’s earlier journey as Prince of Wales had been abandoned; in 1911 the invitation was renewed.

Nepal was not a British colony. The hunt was also an act of diplomacy in which the Rana government displayed its power to assemble animals, specialists, camps and an immense royal entourage. The British monarch was the celebrated guest, but the event depended on Nepalese political authority and local knowledge. This was neither an ordinary private outing nor a spectacle organised by the king alone.

How the hunt was made possible

The hunting technique assembled elephants into a ring around dense vegetation where a tiger was thought to be hiding. Mahouts guided the animals, shikaris and other specialists organised the search, and the formation could gradually restrict the quarry’s movement before trained elephants entered the enclosure. The royal visitor occupied a privileged position within a system prepared and maintained by many people.

Accounts of the visit describe camps at Sukhibar and Kasra. The official record reports nearly 600 elephants in a ring on 25 December 1911; that figure belongs to a particular day, not necessarily to the occasion caught in this photograph. The image alone cannot establish the date, the number of elephants beyond its borders or the precise position of the prey.

What this photograph actually records

Within the pale archival mount, George V stands in an elevated howdah. A working rider sits forward near the elephant’s head, while other elephants and mounted figures appear beyond. The king is visually prominent; the rider’s name is not preserved in the source used here. No tiger is visible. This is a photograph of an active hunting arrangement, not a photograph of the trophy after the killing. It gives us an elevated ruler and the apparatus that makes his position possible, without identifying all the people on whom that apparatus depends.

Who made—and kept—the royal image?

Photographs of the visit were gathered into albums that could circulate beyond Nepal. The Australian National University holds fifty photographs in an album bearing the name of the Herzog & Higgins studio; its individual prints were numbered but not originally captioned. Studio association and album publication do not identify the person who pressed the shutter for this precise image. Ernest Brooks was a photographer associated with the royal party, but his portrayal as the camera operator in the colour reconstruction is an artistic attribution, not a verified credit. Nor has the pencilled “156” on the supplied photograph been securely matched to an individual album record.

The Royal Ring therefore reconstructs more than a hunting formation. It asks how Nepalese hosts, handlers and other workers helped create a royal occasion—and how photographers and publishers transformed that occasion into an image of prestige.

Photographic history · The photograph's second life

From royal hunt to diplomatic gift

December 1911George V's hunt hosted by the Rana government in Nepal
1911–12Photographs gathered in several numbered royal-shoot albums
15 July 1912Chandra Shumsher's album presentation to Admiral Keppel

The original print is numbered “156,” but its precise place in the surviving album tradition and its individual photographer have not been established. The ANU's Herzog & Higgins album contains numbered photographs without period descriptive captions; its present-day descriptions should not be presented as words written in 1911.

The images of the hunt circulated as political keepsakes as well as records of sport. A separately documented album was given by Chandra Shumsher to Admiral Sir Colin Keppel with a letter dated 15 July 1912. The gift commemorated the relationship between a royal guest's party and its Nepalese host.

That Keppel album does not establish that this exact numbered exposure was included. Contemporary critics also questioned the wider practice of big-game hunting, but no verified response to this specific image has been found.
