Out of Frame / The Making of a Trophy

The Making of a Trophy

There is something unsettling about a photograph of a dead animal surrounded by smiling men.

The animal is often magnificent: a tiger, an elephant, a rhinoceros, a buffalo. Its size and strength are part of what makes the photograph remarkable. Its death is the reason the picture exists.

The men stand beside it, above it, or behind it. Sometimes they hold rifles. Sometimes they are accompanied by guides, fellow hunters or an elaborate entourage. The photograph records an occasion considered worthy of preservation: a successful hunt.

To contemporary eyes, these images can seem strangely contradictory. They celebrate the destruction of the very thing whose beauty and power made it desirable. What was once presented as courage, sporting achievement or imperial prestige may now provoke sadness, disbelief or revulsion.

Yet these photographs are more than records of changing attitudes towards animals. They offer a window into how power was performed, how achievements were manufactured, and how certain versions of history were made visible while others were pushed to the margins.

Out of Frame: The Making of a Trophy begins with these images and asks what happens when we look beyond their borders.

The first shot

For centuries, hunting served purposes extending far beyond obtaining food.

Among rulers and political elites, the pursuit of dangerous animals could demonstrate wealth, courage, physical prowess and command over both people and nature. Organising an elaborate hunt required territory, access, equipment, specialised knowledge and substantial human labour. Participation could reinforce social relationships, establish political obligations and confer prestige.

In the age of empire, hunting also became a language of authority.

For European royalty travelling through Asia and Africa, the hunt offered an opportunity to demonstrate power in unfamiliar landscapes. For Indian maharajas and Nepalese rulers, hosting prestigious hunting parties could express their own authority, resources, diplomatic relationships and political standing. For prominent Americans, including Theodore Roosevelt, hunting could become entangled with ideas of exploration, masculine self-reliance, scientific collecting and national identity.

These were not identical traditions, and their participants did not all possess the same power or pursue the same objectives. But they shared an understanding that the encounter between a prominent hunter and an extraordinary animal could carry meaning beyond the event itself.

The more formidable the animal, the greater the achievement might appear. The animal's strength made the hunter's triumph legible. And so a living creature became a trophy.

The second shot

The rifle was not the only instrument involved in making a trophy. There was also the camera.

As photography became increasingly accessible to wealthy travellers, royal households, expeditions and illustrated publications, the hunt acquired a second life. What had been a fleeting event could now become a durable image, preserved in albums, circulated among acquaintances, reproduced in newspapers or eventually deposited in institutional collections.

The photograph did more than establish that an animal had been killed. It gave the event a composition.

A hunter could stand at the centre. His companions could arrange themselves around the carcass. The animal could be displayed so that its size and formidable character remained unmistakable. The resulting image converted an encounter into a public statement of accomplishment.

Even photographs taken before the killing—or during the movement of an expedition—could participate in the same commemorative story. A royal hunter standing in his howdah, a procession of elephants, or a party assembling for the day's sport might signify the scale and prestige of the undertaking.

These images were not necessarily false. Their subjects, animals and equipment may have been exactly as recorded. But a photograph can be accurate within its borders while leaving the larger circumstances unexplained.

Who decided where everyone should stand? Who prepared the animal for display? Who carried the equipment, guided the party or made the journey possible? Who operated the camera? And what happened before the photographer released the shutter?

The photographic frame made certain people and actions central. Others became incidental, anonymous or entirely absent. Over time, those choices could shape what later generations understood the event to have been.

The people beyond the frame

Consider the extraordinary organisation required for an elite big-game hunt.

A celebrated hunter might depend on trackers who understood the behaviour of animals, guides who knew the terrain, attendants who managed weapons, porters who moved supplies, and specialists who maintained the expedition's equipment and camp.

In Nepal, King George V's December 1911 hunting expedition involved an elaborate operation using elephants and skilled handlers. The royal shooting party was hosted by Chandra Shumsher Jung Bahadur Rana, Nepal's powerful prime minister, whose role reminds us that the hunt was also an encounter between political authorities.

Nepalese mahouts controlled the elephants. Shikaris contributed knowledge of the quarry and terrain. Attendants, organisers and other workers sustained the enterprise. Their involvement was essential to a spectacle whose most famous participant was the British king.

The surviving photographs preserve glimpses of these people, sometimes prominently. But their names, individual expertise and experiences have not always survived with the same clarity as those of the royal guests.

Historical invisibility does not always mean someone was physically excluded from a photograph. A person might appear clearly in the image and still remain unidentified in its caption, unmentioned in the accompanying account, or forgotten in the story later told about the event.

The camera recorded their presence without necessarily preserving their history.

A similar question arises in the photographs of Roosevelt's African expedition of 1909–1910. Its celebrated protagonist travelled with naturalists, photographers, professional hunters, guides, gun-bearers, porters and others whose contributions made the enterprise possible. The expedition combined sport, exploration and scientific collecting, with different purposes and consequences that deserve examination on their own terms.

How much of what appears to be an individual accomplishment was, in fact, the product of a larger collective undertaking?

The animal at the centre

There is another absence in many accounts of these photographs: the animal's life before it became a trophy.

In the image, the animal is often presented primarily through its death. Its size becomes evidence of difficulty overcome. Its body becomes a measure of success. Its horns, skin, skull or preserved remains may continue their journey into private collections, museums or other spaces of commemoration.

But the animal was not created for this encounter. It lived within an ecological world of its own, independent of the hunter's reputation and the photographer's intentions.

The historical record often tells us much more about the person who killed an animal than about the animal itself. We may know a hunter's name, title, weapon or tally of kills, while knowing little about the individual creature whose death made the photograph possible.

The distinction between scientific collecting and recreational hunting also requires care. Some expeditions produced specimens and information that contributed to scientific institutions. Others were principally organised for sport, ceremony or prestige. Often these purposes overlapped. Their coexistence does not erase the scientific work, nor does it make the ethical questions surrounding the killing disappear.

What is revealing is the ease with which an animal's death could be transformed into a human achievement. The trophy photograph invites us to admire the hunter because the animal was formidable. It rarely asks us to consider the animal except as proof of that accomplishment.

The third shot

Out of Frame introduces a third act of image-making.

Each artwork begins with an identifiable historical photograph. The original image remains intact and visible, preserving the record that has reached us. Around it, the frame expands.

Through archival research, related photographs, eyewitness accounts and historical reconstruction, the surrounding space becomes an opportunity to investigate what the original composition did not explain.

The expanded image may reveal an expedition's logistical apparatus, the handlers controlling its animals, the political host who organised a royal visit, or a photographer participating in the construction of its enduring visual record.

These surrounding scenes are not presented as newly discovered photographs. They are artistic reconstructions, informed by historical evidence and accompanied by disclosures about what is documented and what remains conjectural.

A person known to have participated in an expedition cannot automatically be placed with certainty just beyond the edge of a particular exposure. An imagined gesture, a reconstructed landscape or an inferred camera position must not be confused with an archival fact.

The aim is not to replace one authoritative-looking image with another, larger one. It is to make the limits of the surviving image visible. The reconstruction does not claim to recover everything that happened. It asks us to reconsider what we thought we knew from the photograph alone.

What we choose to remember

Historical photographs are often treated as neutral windows onto the past. Yet the decision to make, preserve, caption, collect and circulate an image is itself part of history.

Why were these hunting moments photographed so carefully? Why did some become treasured albums, souvenirs or institutional records? Why were the achievements of particular people commemorated while the names of others disappeared?

What did their original audiences admire? And what does our discomfort with these pictures reveal about the distance between their values and our own?

There is no reason to assume that everyone who witnessed these hunts understood them in the same way. Nor should we imagine that concern for animals, criticism of hunting or disagreement about imperial power emerged only in the present. Historical attitudes were never entirely uniform.

But the photographs nevertheless show us what certain powerful individuals and institutions considered worth preserving. They are artifacts of commemoration.

And commemoration is never simply about what happened. It is also about who and what a society decides deserves to be remembered.

Out of Frame invites viewers to encounter these images twice: first as the historical spectacles they were made to record, and then as parts of larger stories involving labour, political authority, animal life and the construction of prestige.

The original photograph remains. What changes is how we look at it.

The hunt made a trophy. The photograph made a monument. The expanded frame asks who—and what—that monument left out.
