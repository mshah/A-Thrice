Out of Frame / Photographic histories

After the shutter

Five men stand behind three dead buffalo heads. A king rises above the grass in an elephant-mounted howdah. A tiger lies before a group of hunters, with elephants towering behind.

The three photographs were made in different political worlds: an American former president's expedition, a British monarch's hunt hosted by Nepal's ruler, and an Indian maharaja's hunting party. Each records a real scene—and each presents a particular version of what was worth remembering.

The hunt ended. The photograph remained. But the shutter was not the final act: someone selected the print, gave it words, assembled an album, sold a publication or preserved an archive. Those later acts helped decide what the photograph would mean.

The second sitting

For many big-game hunters, killing an animal was followed by a second, carefully arranged activity: photographing the result. The animal might be positioned to show its scale, and people assembled around it. Weapons, skins, tents and elephants could become part of the composition. The photograph did more than prove that something had died. It presented the death as an accomplishment.

By the nineteenth century, hunting photographers were already making formal trophy tableaux, alongside other images that showed preparation, skinning and travel. A posed scene could be an accurate photograph of an arrangement without being an unmediated view of the kill itself.

The hunt and the photograph were different operations. The photographer's vantage point, the arrangement of bodies and the choice of the people to include all affected the image. But the photograph seldom tells us who gave instructions—or why a particular person was absent.

A caption is a second frame

Once printed, a photograph acquired another frame: its description. A caption might name a king or president, record a kill count, identify a species, or locate an event. A person could be visible in the image yet remain anonymous in the words below it. A hunter could become the protagonist of a scene that depended on many people.

The distinctions are particularly important in archives. An inscription on the original print, a period magazine caption and a modern catalogue description belong to different moments. We should not read the language of the twenty-first-century catalogue back into the mouths of the first viewers.

Buffalo Corner: from expedition to celebrity

In May 1909, Theodore Roosevelt visited Hugh Heatley's ranch in British East Africa. The surviving trophy photograph shows five named men behind three buffalo heads. It was a staged group portrait, but the person who arranged the group and operated the camera has not been established. The Library of Congress credits Kermit Roosevelt even though he appears among the sitters.

Roosevelt's African journey also became a publishing event. In February 1910, Scribner's Magazine printed A Buffalo-Hunt by the Kamiti, including photographs of the animals, of porters dancing on departure and of naturalist Edmund Heller preparing game heads for shipment. Aspects of the expedition's labour therefore did reach contemporary readers, even while Roosevelt's celebrity and first-person narrative organised the story.

There is an important date to keep separate: the selected five-man photograph survives with a 14 May 1919 copyright-deposit record naming Charles Scribner's Sons. That is documentary evidence about the print's registration, not the date the men stood for the camera. Its first publication has not yet been verified. The exact five-man portrait should not be identified as one of the February 1910 illustrations without an exposure-level match.

The Royal Ring: the photograph as diplomatic gift

In December 1911, George V hunted in Nepal's Tarai as a guest of Chandra Shumsher Jung Bahadur Rana. The selected photograph does not depict a post-kill trophy pose. It shows a monarch elevated in an elephant-mounted howdah during the hunt, with handlers and other mounted participants visible. It is a view of an organised event rather than an isolated act of royal courage.

The photographs of the hunt had a second life in albums. A surviving Herzog & Higgins album at the Australian National University contains 50 numbered photographs without period descriptive captions; the library supplied the descriptions now used online. Other sets circulated through presentation collections and photographic agencies.

One album's route is unusually well documented: Chandra Shumsher presented a photographic album to Admiral Sir Colin Keppel, accompanied by a letter dated 15 July 1912. The gift commemorated a personal and diplomatic encounter. It does not establish that our exact numbered photograph appeared in that recipient's album.

The Host and the Trophy: who owns the image?

The Pollen album, associated with Maharaja Nripendra Narayan of Cooch Behar in 1895–96, contained 166 photographs, many captioned by hand. The compiler's name does not tell us who operated the camera for the chosen tiger-group exposure. Its particular handwritten caption and intended first audience have not been recovered.

Here princely authority becomes central. The maharaja was an organiser and host of international shooting parties, not simply a supporting figure in someone else's story. His own illustrated hunting memoir later circulated in the British sporting-book market.

The royal household also challenged the subsequent ownership of images. In her 1921 autobiography, Maharani Suniti Devi complained that an English author had reproduced photographs of Cooch Behar shooting camps while claiming credit for them. We cannot assume she meant the Pollen photograph or its compiler. Her testimony nevertheless shows that photographic authorship was already contested by people represented in the colonial-era visual record.

The printed image could change again

Even after an exposure was made, a publisher could change its presentation. One unusually clear example is a photograph by Peter Burges showing a dead rhinoceros and elephants during a hunting party associated with Cooch Behar.

The two prints use different crop boundaries and captions. One describes elephants gathering around a newly shot rhinoceros; the other labels it simply A Dead Rhinoceros. The fuller upper view in 1900 does not prove that it shows the entire negative. Nor do the altered boundaries reveal the publisher's motives.

Not everyone admired the picture

It is tempting to imagine an age of unquestioning admiration for the big-game trophy photograph, followed by a recent age of criticism. Contemporary publishing records suggest something less simple.

Roosevelt's expedition photographs and prose were promoted to magazine readers as adventure and natural history. The official narrative of George V's Nepal hunt commemorated royal sport. An early 1910 review of Nripendra Narayan's illustrated memoir acknowledged its interest to sportsmen while judging its scientific contribution limited.

In Killing for Sport, published in the 1910s, Ernest Bell criticised the repetitive conventions of big-game hunting illustrations and the treatment of animals. He also drew on the official royal-visit narrative to question the use of tethered bullocks as tiger bait. Bell's critique addressed hunting imagery and the Nepal hunt generally; it is not documented as a response to the exact photograph used in The Royal Ring.

These surviving responses show more than one reading community: sporting enthusiasts, scientific readers, political guests and humanitarian critics. Distribution can be demonstrated more often than individual reception. It would be wrong to assign a common reaction to everyone who appeared in, worked on or viewed these photographs.

The last frame

The photographs survived the events they commemorated. Many of their subjects were named; many others remained anonymous. Their captions were copied and rewritten. Some prints were given to distinguished guests; others reached commercial readers; some entered archives that added another layer of description.

These are authentic records of selected moments, but their authority was never made by the camera alone. It grew through arrangement, publication, ownership and the work of memory.

Out of Frame begins with the surviving image and makes a further, disclosed intervention. The historical rectangle stays intact. The expanded colour field is a contemporary reconstruction: it does not pretend to recover a lost wider negative or return all unseen people to one proven instant.

The hunt made the trophy. The photograph made it visible. Its circulation helped make it a monument. What else deserves to be remembered?
